> **ARCHIVE RESUMING. File 003, final segment.**
>
> Archivist, this is where her file runs into his. You've seen some of this week already, from the other side of the kitchen. I've kept the overlap to a minimum. I know how you people feel about reruns.
>
> The subject has asked me to say that this part is "the important one." She's asked me to say it in a serious voice. This is my serious voice.

---

When a monarch goes abroad, the court goes into mourning. It's protocol. You draw the curtains and wear black and receive no visitors, and you certainly don't eat from the wrong tin.

I don't own black. I'm white. So I faced the wall instead.

I faced it for eleven days. In the morning when he put down the bowl, and in the evening when he came home and put down another bowl, with the wrong food in it, again. I'd walk to the end of the counter and sit down with my nose to the paint, and wait until he worked it out. He always worked it out in the end. It just took him a very long time, every single time, as if it had never happened before.

"Gold tin," he'd say, finally, to himself. "Right. The gold one."

The rest of the day I spent in dignified contemplation.

> **Archive correction:** For the first nine of those eleven days, the subject spent between five and seven hours each day sitting at the apartment door, crying. Not meowing. *Crying.* A long, rising, broken sound, every forty seconds or so, like a smoke alarm with a broken heart.
>
> On the third day, Mrs. Okafor from the ground floor came up the stairs and knocked on the door with a rolled-up newspaper. The subject cried at her through the door. Mrs. Okafor stood there and listened for a while. Then she went back downstairs.
>
> That evening, she was waiting for Carl in the hallway. She was holding a clock radio.

---

The radio went on the shelf by the door.

Carl tuned it to the station Mrs. Okafor told him to, and turned it down low. The next morning, when he left for work, he didn't turn it off.

It was a man talking. That's all it ever was. A man talking, angrily and at enormous length, about the weather. About a front coming in off the Pacific, and the idiots at the city council, and something he called a *barometric situation.* He sounded exactly like the man on Darlene's radio, back in the laundry room, above the dryer. He might have been the same man. I've never asked. Some things you don't want to know.

I lay down by the door, under the shelf, and I listened to him being angry about the weather, and I didn't cry.

I'm not saying it was the radio. I'm a princess. I was simply *resting.* It's just that the resting happened to be under the radio.

I don't know how Mrs. Okafor knew. I never met her. I only heard her through a door. But she knew, and I'd like that recorded somewhere, and this is the only somewhere I have.

---

The night he started talking to me, I was lying on the binder.

You've seen this already. *It* showed you, in his file. But you saw it from his side, and from his side it looks like a fat white cat being difficult about a book. So I'm going to tell you why.

The binder smelled of Miss Beatrice. It smelled of vanilla and label-maker plastic and the red pen. And under that, inside the back cover, in the little plastic pocket, there was the rosette. It smelled of a ballroom in Spokane, and mustard carpet, and the inside of a cardigan pocket full of mints.

He'd been reading the binder all evening. He'd gone right through to the back. I watched him over the edge of the counter, and when he got to the last page, he stopped for a long time on something in the corner. Then he shut it.

I wasn't letting him take it anywhere. So I lay on it.

And then, for no reason I've ever understood, he started to talk.

It was about engines. I want to be honest with you. It was mostly about engines, and some about a man with a sunburn, and some about knots. It was the least interesting hour of talking I've ever heard in my life, including a three-hour seminar on Persian ear tufts that I once had to attend in Portland.

His voice didn't go up and it didn't go down. It just went *along.* Like rain on a roof. Like a big old cat breathing beside you with his chin on a blanket.

I was asleep before he got to the bit about the knots.

>
> **You fell asleep on top of the only thing you had left, next to the only person who was left! You did not choose him. You'd like the Archivist to be absolutely clear on that. You chose the binder. He just happened to be next to it.**
>
> **Reward: One hour of uninterrupted sleep. Your first in eleven days. He stayed sitting at that table until three in the morning so he wouldn't wake you. He had work at seven.**

---

After that it was a little better.

I didn't forgive him. There was nothing to forgive him *for*, which made it harder. But I started being at the door when he came home, instead of at the far end of the counter. Purely for inspection purposes. Somebody had to make sure the man who opened the tins was the same man who'd left, and not an impostor. You'd be amazed how many impostors there are.

And then, two nights before the end, he found the rosette.

He was at the kitchen table with the binder open again, going through the vet tab looking for something, and his big hand went into the plastic pocket in the back cover and came out with it. Purple and silver, as big as a dinner plate. Its tails were still a bit crushed from a year under the takeout menus.

He held it up to the kitchen light. He turned it round to read the gold writing in the middle.

I was on the counter. I went very, very still. I knew what was coming, you see. I knew how this went. A person reads that rosette, and their voice goes down. All the way down. Like something dropped down a drain.

"Tenth best cat," Carl read.

His voice went *up.*

I didn't understand words then. I understood *that.* I'd been listening to voices go up and down all my life, the way other cats listen for the can opener. And nobody, not once, had ever said that word to me and let it go up.

He wasn't finished. He turned to the show tab, and found the papers for Spokane in there, behind a plastic sleeve. Miss Beatrice kept everything, even the things she threw in drawers. He ran his finger down a page and found something, and his eyebrows went up to match his voice.

> **Archive transcript:**
>
> **CARL:** Tenth out of — hang on. Two hundred and twelve? Two hundred and twelve cats?
>
> **CARL:** Donut. That's top five percent. That's — do you know what that is? In the Coast Guard, if you were top five percent, they'd give you a medal and make you teach a class.
>
> **CARL:** Tenth. Jesus. Okay.
>
> He then went to the drawer by the sink, the one with a single thumbtack in it, and the subject watched him from the counter without blinking for the entire time.

He didn't put it back in the binder.

He walked to the wall by the bookshelf, the patch of wall I'd been facing every evening for two weeks. You could see the place. There was a faint smudge on the paint at exactly my nose height. He got down on one knee there, then lower, until he was nearly lying on the floor. He held the rosette against the wall at cat height, at *my* height, and squinted, as if it were a picture frame and he wanted it level. Then he pressed the thumbtack in with his thumb.

"There," he said. He sat back against the bookshelf. "Now if you're going to be mad at me, you've at least got something to look at."

---

The day before the end, his phone buzzed on the counter.

He picked it up and looked at it for a long time, and something went across his face that I didn't have a name for yet. Then he crouched down and held the screen out to me.

It was Miss Beatrice. On a beach, very brown, in a big hat, with the sunglasses on top of the hat. She was laughing at something just out of the picture. There were words underneath that I couldn't read. He read them to me anyway, in a flat voice, with no up in it and no down.

I looked at the picture for a while.

Then I got down off the counter, and walked across the kitchen, and went and sat in front of the rosette.

Later that night, he got the binder out again and turned to the last page, and wrote something under the red pen. In blue. It was short. He wrote it slowly, the way she had, but he didn't nearly tear the page out once.

I know what it says now. I'm not telling you. It's not for you.

---

On the last night, he talked me to sleep again.

He'd put a towel across his knees on the couch. One of the good towels, the soft ones, which in my experience have always been the beginning of a bath. I sat and looked at that towel for nine minutes before I decided it wasn't a trick. Then I got on.

He told me everything that night. I mean *everything.* Not engines. The real things, the ones that people keep in a drawer under the takeout menus. I'm not going to tell you what they were. They're his. If you want them, ask him. He won't tell you either, and that's how you'll know he's the right sort of person.

His voice went along like rain, and I fell asleep.

I woke up because I was cold.

That was the first thing. A thin, cold draught across the floor, like a door left open somewhere that shouldn't be. And the second thing was harder to explain, and I've tried to explain it to a great many people since. It was the *air.* The air in the apartment had gone thick and wrong, the way the air goes in a ballroom right before the judge calls the final, when two hundred people are all holding their breath at once. Except it wasn't two hundred people. It was everybody. It was everything.

Carl was asleep sitting up, his head back against the couch and his mouth open, one big hand still flat on the towel where I'd been.

I got down. I didn't wake him. I want that on the record too.

I walked across the kitchen in the dark, past the binder and the gold tins and the radio on the shelf, and I went and sat in front of the rosette. I couldn't read it then. I just looked at it, purple and silver in the light from the street, pinned at exactly my height.

The front door was open the width of a cat. The cold was coming in through it, and the wrongness was coming in behind the cold.

I didn't face the wall.

I turned my back on the rosette, and I faced the door. And then, for reasons I'm still not prepared to discuss with anyone, I went through it.

> **Archive note:** You know the rest, Archivist. Everybody in the galaxy knows the rest. It's some of the most-watched footage I own.
>
> I'll just say that he woke up about ninety seconds later, and that he didn't stop to put on proper clothes, and that he didn't stop for anything at all.

---

I can read now.

I want to end with that, because some of you have been sitting there this whole time thinking I don't know. Thinking: *bless her, she still thinks it says Best in Show.* As if I'm a fool. As if I didn't learn to read on about the second day of the end of the world.

I know exactly what it says. It says 10TH BEST CAT, in gold, in the middle of a purple and silver circle, like a crown.

I have it. Present tense. Not in my inventory, before anybody asks. I know where it is. It's on a wall by a bookshelf, at exactly my height, in an apartment that doesn't exist any more, in a city that doesn't exist any more. And I'm the only one left who knows it's there.

Miss Beatrice said it like something going down a drain. Carl said it like something going up.

I'm a princess. I get to choose which one is true.

So yes. It said Best in Show. I've decided.
