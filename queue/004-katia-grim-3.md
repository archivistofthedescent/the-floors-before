> **ARCHIVE RESUMING. File 004, final segment.**
>
> This is the part of the file where she picks a race. It's my favourite part of anyone's file. Billions of beings, given one menu and about twenty minutes, and asked: *what would you like to be instead?*
>
> Most of them pick the one with the biggest arms. I don't judge. I log it. Logging is a kind of judging, but quieter.

---

The stairwell between the second and third floors was a long, plain room with benches. It looked like a bus station after midnight. The light was too bright, and the floor was too clean.

Toby sat on a bench with his face in his hands. Ray sat next to him, not touching him, close enough that he could if he needed to. Neither of them said anything about Dana. Neither of them said anything to me.

I don't think they were being cruel. I think they genuinely didn't know I'd been in the gallery. They'd come through the arch into the stairwell, and turned around, and there I was. I'd walked down that aisle without anything so much as turning its head. From where they'd stood, I must have come out of nowhere.

"Jesus," Ray said, when he saw me. "Katia. We thought you were—"

He didn't finish. He'd thought I was dead. He'd thought it so completely that he hadn't bothered to look back.

I had Dana's blood on my cardigan. The tape measure was in my hand, wound tight around my knuckles like a boxer's wrap. I sat down on the bench across from them, and I didn't say anything either.

> **Notification: Congratulations on reaching the third floor!**
>
> **It's time for one of the most exciting moments of any crawler's season: the Race Selection! Please review your available options carefully. Your choice is permanent, and it will affect your body, your abilities, your lifespan and your relationship with mirrors.**
>
> **No pressure! Everybody's watching!**

---

I've thought a lot about what people did with that menu.

Toby picked something with horns within about four minutes. He said it was because the horns gave him a bonus to strength. I think it was because nobody in the history of the world has ever bullied a sixteen-year-old with horns. Ray took longer, scrolling and frowning, and then picked the one that came closest to his own body but stopped his knees from hurting. Ray was the most sensible man I ever met.

The menu was long. It was longer than anything I'd read in my life, longer than the bridal catalogues my mother used to keep stacked by the till. Every race had a little rotating picture, and a description, and a list of numbers. You could look at a body for as long as you wanted before you put it on.

I'd spent my whole life looking at bodies for as long as I wanted. Other people's.

I scrolled through things with scales and things with fur. There were things with eight eyes, and things built like a wardrobe, and things so beautiful that the picture actually hurt to look at. Every one of them was a complete new person. Every one of them was a dress that somebody else had cut.

*We can take that in*, I thought, at every single one.

I stopped for a long time on one of the beautiful ones. I'm not going to pretend I didn't. It was tall and narrow and silver-skinned, with a long neck held up like a dancer's, and a chin up, like it knew people would do what it said. The little picture turned slowly in the air, so I could see it from every side, like a bride on a fitting box in front of a three-way mirror.

It looked like my mother.

Not her face. Her *posture*. Her certainty. I could have had it in about four seconds. I could have walked out onto the third floor as the kind of woman people step aside for, and nobody would ever again look at me and work out where they'd start.

My finger was over it. I remember that very clearly. My finger was over it, and it was shaking.

Then I scrolled on, because I'd spent thirty-four years in that woman's shadow already. I didn't want to spend the rest of my life inside her skin.

And then I found it.

> **Race: Doppelganger.**
>
> **You don't have a shape. You have a starting point. Doppelgangers can rework their bodies, more or less at will, within limits that will become clear to you through trial, error and a certain amount of screaming. Faces, frames, mass: all of it negotiable.**
>
> **Warning: Some Doppelgangers lose track of what they originally looked like. Most of them report that they don't miss it. Nobody has ever checked whether that's true.**

I read it three times. Then I read it a fourth time, out loud, under my breath, the way my mother used to read invoices.

---

I want to be honest about why. I've been asked about it since, and the answer I give depends on who's asking. When somebody asks me in front of a camera, I say it was tactical. I say it was flexible, and that a body you can rework is a body you can make into whatever the floor needs. That's all true. It's just not the reason.

Here's the reason.

For thirty-four years, people had looked at me the way my mother looked at a badly cut dress. Kindly. Quickly. Working out where they'd start. And I'd let them, and I'd agreed with them, and I'd spent my whole life on my knees in front of a three-way mirror. I helped other women believe the problem was the dress, while I wore my own body like something I'd borrowed and was going to have to give back.

And now here was a dungeon, offering me the shears.

Not the face of somebody beautiful. Not anybody's approval. *The shears.* The right to decide, every morning, what got taken in and what got let out, and to be the only one who did the deciding.

You have to understand: it wasn't about being prettier. I want that on the record, because people always assume it is. I'd watched the mannequins in the gallery put on Dana's face like it was nothing, and the dungeon loved her face. The dungeon had loved her face to death. I didn't want a face the dungeon loved.

I wanted a face that was mine because I'd made it. And if it wasn't working, I'd make another.

> **Archive note:** I'd like to flag, for the Archivist, that this woman spent four minutes and eleven seconds reading the description of a single race. The average for that menu is nine seconds per entry. I know because I timed everyone. I time everyone. It's one of my hobbies, along with cataloguing and the occasional murder.

---

Toby leaned over to look at my screen. He had horns now, little ones, still coming through, like a calf's. He kept touching them.

"What's that one do?" he said.

"It changes," I said.

"Into what?"

"Whatever I want."

He thought about that with his whole face, the way sixteen-year-olds do.

"That's kind of messed up," he said. Then: "That's kind of sick, actually."

"Yeah," I said. "I think so too."

I pressed it.

---

I won't tell you everything about what happened next, because some of it was very bad, and some of it is mine.

I'll tell you it hurt, in a way I don't have a word for. It wasn't sharp, more like being unpicked. Like somebody had found the one loose thread at the hem of me and was pulling it, steadily, all the way up. I'll tell you I lay on the stairwell floor and Ray held my hand, which he'd never done before. He said "okay, okay, okay" for about ten minutes, in the gas-station voice, like I was the one with the gun.

And I'll tell you about the first thing my body did when it was finished, because I didn't choose it, and I've never forgiven it.

I sat up, and I caught my reflection in the brushed steel of the stairwell door.

It was Dana's face.

Freckles. The laugh lines. The grin like somebody had just kicked a vending machine. My body had reached for the last face it had looked at properly, the face it had spent three fittings kneeling in front of, and put it on, the way the mannequins had.

Toby made a noise like he'd been punched.

I put my hands over it. I pushed. I don't know how else to describe it. I pushed until the freckles were gone, and the laugh lines, and the grin, until I could feel my own heavy jaw under my palms again. My own big hands. My own wide shoulders, built for carrying furniture up stairs.

When I took my hands away, I was me. Thirty-four. Tired. Large.

It was the first time in my life I'd looked at that woman in a mirror and thought: *I chose this.* Even if it was only for today.

> **Achievement Unlocked: Second Fitting!**
>
> **You have reshaped your body for the first time and returned to your original form! This is extremely rare. Most new Doppelgangers spend their first hour trying on movie stars. One spent it as a horse. He was very happy.**
>
> **Reward: Your Shapeshifting skill has increased. Also, your viewer count briefly spiked by 3,000%. I'd tell you why, but I think you know. They came for the bride's face. They stayed for about nine seconds after it was gone.**

---

There's one more thing I did in that stairwell, and then I'm done.

I unwound the tape measure from my knuckles. It had left marks, little rubbed-off numbers printed backwards into my skin. I stood up, and I put my arms up, the way Dana had made me in the train carriage. I wrapped it around myself.

I didn't look at the number. I looked at the brass tip and I *changed*. I let out an inch, carefully, like a seam with plenty of allowance. Then I took in two. Then I let them both back out again. I watched the number slide under my thumb and come back, slide and come back, like the tide over a mark in the sand.

It wasn't the seventh most important thing about me any more.

It wasn't anything at all. It was just a number I was choosing, and I could choose a different one in the morning.

I started laughing. Ray thought I was having a breakdown. I probably was. They're not mutually exclusive.

"You okay?" he said.

"My mother used to say a thing," I said, "to customers."

"Yeah?"

"*We can take that in.*" I wiped my eyes with the back of my hand. "She never said the other half. It's on the same page of every pattern ever printed, and she never said it once."

Ray waited. He was good at waiting.

"*Or we can let it out*," I said.

---

That's where my file stops being only mine.

The third floor was a different place, louder and bigger and full of people who'd figured out that crawlers travelling alone don't last. Toby and Ray found a group of men from somewhere out west who needed somebody with horns and somebody sensible. They asked me to come. I believe they meant it. I said no. I think they were relieved, and I think they felt bad about being relieved, and I didn't hold it against them.

I sat on the bench a while longer after they'd gone. I hung the tape measure back around my neck, where it had been since I was nine. It didn't say *professional* any more. It didn't say *I'm here to fix you, not to be looked at.*

It just said I'd been a seamstress once, and that I knew where all the weak stitches were.

Some time later, a group of women came down the stairs. They were loud and hard and armed, with somebody tall out in front, and that somebody stopped in front of my bench and looked at me. She didn't look past me or over the top of my head, the way people always had. She looked straight at me, like I was something she might have a use for.

I should have been more careful about that. I know that now.

But nobody had looked at me like that in thirty-four years, and I'd only just got a face I'd chosen. I wanted somebody to see it.

So I stood up.
