> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 2 DAYS.**

The thing nobody tells you about the end of the world is that the second-to-last day is a Tuesday. Or whatever. It's a day. You go to work.

It rained, finally. The sky had been threatening it all week, and on the second day before the end it gave up and came down sideways off the water. It got into everything. The dock planks went black and slick. Lyle stood in the doorway of the shop office in a yellow slicker, looking out at it like it owed him money.

"Small craft advisory," he said. "Nobody's taking anything out today. Go home if you want."

"I'll stay."

"Suit yourself." He watched me rebuild a carburettor for a while. Lyle was not a man who watched people work. He was a man who watched the weather and occasionally the Mariners. So I knew something was coming.

"Can I ask you something," he said.

"You're going to anyway."

"Why'd you get out?" He nodded at me, at my hands, at the whole of me. "Coast Guard. You're good. You're the best mechanic I've had in twenty years and you don't even like boats. Why'd you get out?"

I set the float bowl down on the bench. I lined it up with the edge, square. Then I wiped my hands on a rag for longer than they needed it.

"Enlistment was up," I said.

Lyle waited.

"That's it. Enlistment was up."

He nodded slowly, the Lyle nod. He'd done it when I told him about the cat. He'd done it when I told him about the boots. I was starting to understand that it didn't mean *I believe you.* It meant *okay, I'll wait.*

He went back to looking at the rain.

Here's what I didn't tell Lyle.

Three years in, we got a call in the middle of a January night. A crab boat was taking on water forty miles out, in seas that were bad even for those guys. By the time our cutter got there, the boat was on its side. There were lights in the water. Little strobes on survival suits, blinking white, going up and down on swells taller than a house.

I was deck crew. I was clipped in at the rail, wearing those boots, which were new then and still stiff and gave me blisters on both heels. I remember that. I remember the blisters more clearly than some of the faces. That's the kind of thing your brain does when it's busy.

We got four of them out with the rescue swimmer and the basket. That left two. A man and a kid. The kid was nineteen, it turned out. His father was the captain. They'd tied themselves together with a length of line, which is either the smartest or the dumbest thing you can do in that water. I've gone back and forth on it for years.

The swell pushed them in against our hull. The kid came up right underneath me, close enough to touch. I went flat on the deck, reached through the rail, and got a fist in the collar of his suit.

And then I just held on.

That's all I did. I want to be clear. I didn't do anything brave. I didn't jump in. I lay on a steel deck in the dark, with sea water coming over me every eight seconds like somebody emptying a bathtub on my head, and I held a nineteen-year-old by the collar. The cutter rolled and the line between him and his father went tight, then slack, then tight.

Four minutes, they told me later. It felt like an hour. It felt like the rest of my life.

Then the line went slack and stayed slack.

I don't know if the knot failed, or if the father cut it. He had a knife on his belt. Everybody on those boats does. I've thought about that knife more than I've thought about anything else in my life. I've decided, at different times, every possible thing about it.

They got the kid up in the basket. They never found the father.

The kid didn't thank me. I don't blame him. He looked at me as they wrapped him up, a long flat look, and I knew exactly what he was thinking, because I was thinking it too.

*You had hold of the wrong one.*

My mother died three weeks after that.

I was at sea when it happened. By the time the message reached the cutter it was two days old, and she'd already been gone for three. I'm not going to tell you how she died. It wasn't him, not directly, not in any way a court would care about. But I'd known her house for eighteen years, and I'd left it for the Coast Guard at the first possible chance. I knew what living in it for another four years without me would have cost her.

I got leave. I flew home. I went straight from the airport to the funeral home in my uniform and the stiff new boots, with the blisters on both heels.

My father was there.

He was sober. That was the worst part, if I'm honest. I'd prepared myself for the other version. I'd spent the whole flight getting ready for a loud man in a bad suit who'd make a scene, so that I'd have a reason to make one back. Instead there was a quiet, grey, shrunken guy in the front row, with his hands folded, looking at the casket like he didn't understand how it had got there.

He looked up when I came in. He looked at my uniform, and then at my boots.

"Those are good boots," he said.

That's the only thing he said to me. Those are good boots.

I didn't answer him. I walked past him and sat down at the far end of the front row, with three empty chairs between us. I stayed there for the whole service, and at the end I left without a word, and I haven't spoken to him since. I don't know where he is. I don't know if he's alive. I've had people tell me that I should find out, that I'll regret it. Maybe they're right.

But I'll tell you what I worked out on the flight back to the cutter.

I'd been holding on to the wrong one my whole life. I'd been holding onto my anger at him, keeping it in my fist like a collar in the water, and I'd let go of her. I'd let the line go slack and told myself the knot had failed.

So I made a rule. I didn't write it down. I'm not a rule guy. But I made it.

*You don't let go of the one who's in front of you.*

Not when it's inconvenient. Not when they bite you. Not when they're ungrateful, or when someone else would be easier, or when everyone around you is telling you to drop it. The one who's in front of you is the one you've got.

I didn't re-enlist. I wanted a job where nobody could ship me forty miles from the people I was supposed to be holding onto. Enlistment was up, though. That part was true.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 1 DAY.**

Bea texted back.

It came in at six in the morning, while I was combing the cat. Wide tooth first, then fine, the towel across my knees. She'd stopped going rigid. She'd started lying down on the towel before I'd finished spreading it out, like a customer who'd booked the appointment.

The phone buzzed on the floor next to me. I saw her name and my heart did something stupid.

It was a photo of a beach. A white one, with a drink on a table in the foreground, the kind with a little umbrella in it. The sky was so blue it looked fake. Under the photo she'd written: *omg tell my baby mama misses her!!! kiss her face for me*

That was all of it.

I read it a couple of times. I can't tell you how many. Not forty. Less than forty.

It didn't say when she was coming back. It didn't say anything about me. It wasn't cruel, either. I want to be fair to her, because I think it's important to be fair to people even when you don't want to be. It was just a message from someone on a beach, to the guy who happened to be standing near her cat.

I put the phone face down on the floor.

"She says hi," I told Donut.

Donut opened one eye. She didn't seem to care. She wanted the fine-tooth comb. I picked it up and kept going, and after a minute the idling-engine noise started up again, low.

I kissed her face for Bea, because I'd been asked to. Then I kissed it once more for me, because nobody was watching.

> **Correction:** I was watching.

---

That night I looked at the picture on the wall.

It hung by the kitchen door. It was a big framed photo of Bea and the cat at some show, both of them wearing ribbons. Bea was laughing, with her head back. Donut was looking at the camera with the face of a champion who'd never been told no in her life.

It was the picture from the hardware store. That's where the whole thing started. She'd come up to me in the fasteners aisle, holding a frame exactly like this one and a little packet of screws, and she'd asked me if they'd hold. And I'd said *depends how heavy the picture is.* I'd come over that weekend with a stud finder and hung it for her.

It had been on that wall for a year and a half. It hadn't moved a quarter of an inch. I'd done a good job. I always do a good job with things that hang on walls. It's people I have trouble with.

I stood there with my hands in my pockets and thought about taking it down. Not in a dramatic way. I wasn't going to smash it. I just thought maybe I'd take it down, wrap it in a towel and put it in the closet, so I didn't have to walk past Bea laughing every time I went to get a beer.

Donut came and sat by my feet. She looked up at the picture. She looked up at me.

"Yeah," I said. "I know. It's you, too."

I left it up.

It turns out it depends how heavy the picture is, and some of them are heavier than they look. You put the screw in properly, and into a stud, not the drywall. Then you leave it alone, and it holds.

The cobbler's ticket was on the fridge, under a magnet shaped like a crab. *Ready Thursday*, in the neat old-man cursive.

Tomorrow was Thursday.
