> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 7 DAYS.**

The cat had been mine for eleven days, and she'd hated me for all eleven of them.

That's not fair. Hate takes effort. She didn't put effort into me. She looked at me the way you'd look at a dishwasher that wasn't loading itself correctly. Like I was an appliance, and a disappointing one.

Her name, according to the paperwork Bea left on the kitchen counter, was Princess Donut. She was a Persian, she had a pedigree longer than my entire family tree, and she'd won things. Actual things, with ribbons. There was a binder. The binder had tabs.

The note on top of the binder said *Back soon. Don't let her eat anything off the floor. —B.*

It did not say when *soon* was. It did not say where Bea had gone, or whether I was still her boyfriend, or just a guy with a key and a functioning fridge. I'd read the note maybe forty times by then. It never said anything new.

"Breakfast," I told the cat.

Princess Donut looked at the bowl. She looked at me. Then she walked very slowly to the far end of the counter, sat down facing the wall, and stayed there until I put the good food in instead. The good food came in a tiny gold tin that cost more than my lunch.

"You know I'm the one who buys that, right?" I said. "With money. From a job."

She didn't turn around. But I swear her tail flicked once, like *noted*.

---

The job was at a marina on the south side, fixing outboard motors for guys who called them "the boat" the way other guys said "the wife". Four years in the Coast Guard had taught me three things that turned out to be useful in civilian life. Engines. Knots. And how to look like I was listening while somebody with a lot of money explained something I already knew.

My boss was a man named Lyle. He had a sunburn that had been going since 1997.

"Carl," said Lyle, "you look like hell."

"Cat," I said.

"You got a cat?"

"I have custody of a cat. Temporarily."

Lyle nodded slowly, like I'd told him I was going through something. Maybe I was. He handed me a coffee that tasted like it had been brewed in the bilge. Then he pointed at my boots.

"Those are done, son."

He wasn't wrong. They were my old service boots. Steel toe, black leather, laces I'd replaced so many times they didn't match. Somewhere in the last month, the left sole had started separating from the upper at the toe. When I walked, it flapped. Not a lot, just enough. Like the boot was trying to say something and kept losing its nerve.

"They're fine," I said.

"They're talking, Carl."

"They're broken in."

"They're broken *out*." He took a sip of his own coffee and grimaced. "There's a cobbler on Fifth. Old guy. He'll fix 'em if they're worth fixing."

I looked down at the boots. I'd stood in those boots on the deck of a cutter in a swell so big I'd had to clip in. I'd stood in those boots at a funeral. I'd been wearing them the day Bea and I met, in a hardware store, when she'd asked me whether a particular screw would hold a picture frame, and I'd said "depends how heavy the picture is," like an idiot. She'd laughed anyway.

"They're worth fixing," I said.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 6 DAYS.**

The cobbler on Fifth was an old guy, like Lyle said. Older than old. He had magnifying glasses clipped to his regular glasses and hands like walnut roots. His shop smelled of leather and glue and the radiator that ticked in the corner, and he didn't say hello when I came in. He just held out his hand.

I gave him the boots.

He turned the left one over. He bent the sole, sniffed it, and actually sniffed it, and put it down.

"Military," he said.

"Coast Guard."

"Still military." He tapped the toe where the sole was coming away. "This is fixable. Resole, new welt, new laces. A week."

"A week?"

"You have somewhere to be?"

I thought about it. Work, and the apartment, and the cat facing the wall.

"No," I said. "Guess not."

He wrote me a ticket on a little paper stub with a number on it. *Ready Thursday*, he wrote, in neat old-man cursive. Then he looked up at me over both pairs of glasses.

"You walk like a man carrying something," he said.

"I walked in here carrying two boots."

He didn't laugh. "Thursday," he said again, and turned back to his bench.

I walked home in my sneakers, which felt wrong. Too light. Like I'd left something behind.

---

That night the cat sat on the binder.

I'd left it open on the kitchen table, because I'd been reading it. Not the tabs about grooming or show schedules. The part at the very back, where Bea had written notes to herself in different pens over different years. *Donut likes the blue blanket, not the grey one.* *Donut is afraid of the vacuum, but will NEVER admit it.* *Donut sleeps better if someone talks to her. Doesn't matter what about.*

And, in one corner, in red pen, small: *Carl is good with her. Better than me.*

I didn't know what to do with that, so I closed the binder and the cat climbed straight onto it and lay down. She stretched out across the cover like a fat, furious paperweight.

"I wasn't done," I said.

Princess Donut yawned. She had a lot of teeth for something so fluffy.

I sat there for a while. Then, because there was nothing else to do and the apartment was very quiet, I started talking. I told her about the cutter, and about Lyle's sunburn. I told her about the cobbler who'd sniffed my boot, and about the hardware store, and the screw, and depends how heavy the picture is.

At some point her eyes closed.

At some point I realised I'd been talking for an hour to a cat, and the cat had been listening. Or at least sleeping near me on purpose, which, for her, might have been the same thing.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 5 DAYS.**

Here is what I remember about the fifth day before the end of the world. I remember it so clearly that it makes me angry.

I remember the sky was low and grey and smelled like rain that wasn't coming. I remember a guy at the marina dropped his phone in the water, and I fished it out with a net, and he tipped me twenty bucks and said "you're a lifesaver." Ha. I remember Mrs. Okafor from the ground-floor apartment yelling at the mailman about a package. I remember the mailman yelling back. I remember thinking *this neighbourhood, man.*

I remember stopping outside the cobbler's on the way home and looking through the window. My boots were on his bench with the sole off, pinned in a clamp. They looked naked like that, like a dog after a bath.

I remember getting home and the cat was waiting by the door.

Not facing the wall. Not at the far end of the counter. By the door, sitting up very straight, the way she probably sat for judges.

"Hey," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she turned around and walked to her bowl and sat next to it, pointedly.

It wasn't affection. I knew that. It was a business arrangement.

But she'd been waiting by the door.
