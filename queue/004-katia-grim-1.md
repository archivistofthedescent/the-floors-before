My mother had a phrase. She said it to customers, and she said it to me, and it took me about twenty-five years to notice that it was the same phrase.

*We can take that in.*

She said it kneeling, with pins in her mouth, so it came out soft and a little lisped. A woman would come out of the changing room in a dress that didn't fit and turn sideways in front of the three-way mirror, sucking in her stomach and hating herself in triplicate. My mother would look at her for exactly one second and say it. *Oh, honey. We can take that in.* And the woman would breathe out, because somebody had told her the problem was the dress.

When I was fourteen, I came down for breakfast in a skirt I'd bought with my own money. My mother looked at me for exactly one second, over her coffee.

"We can take that in," she said.

She meant the skirt. I think she meant the skirt. I've spent a long time deciding to believe she meant the skirt.

---

The shop was called Katia's Alterations, and it had been called that since before I was born. My mother named it after me while I was still a kick in her side. Our last name is Grim. She said nobody in their right mind lets a woman named Grim near their wedding dress, so the sign got my first name, in curly pink letters, with an apostrophe she had to pay extra for.

I grew up under the cutting table. It was in a strip mall off the highway, between a nail salon and a place that sold vape cartridges and phone cases and, for one confusing summer, live crickets. The sign out front buzzed. The parking lot had one working light. Inside, it smelled of steam, sizing spray and the burnt-dust smell of an iron that's been on since six in the morning.

I learned to thread a needle before I learned to read. By twelve I could do a blind hem. By sixteen I was doing bridal bustles while my mother did the talking, because she was better at the talking.

My mother was a beautiful woman. That isn't a complaint. It's just a fact I'm putting on the table so you understand the rest of it.

She was tiny and sharp and dark-eyed, and people did what she said. She'd been a dancer before she was a seamstress, and she held herself like one, her neck long and her chin up. I came out of her large. Large baby, large child, big hands, wide shoulders, a body built for carrying furniture up stairs. A body built for a different woman's life.

She never said anything cruel. I want to be fair to her. She never once said anything you could write down.

She just looked at me sometimes, kindly, the way she looked at a dress that had come in from a department store with the seams all wrong. She was working out where she'd start.

---

I wore a tape measure around my neck from the time I was nine.

It was hers first: a soft vinyl tape, sixty inches long, gone yellow-grey with use, with the numbers half rubbed off in the middle where everybody's waist lives. The brass tip at the zero end was bent from being stepped on. She bought a new one when I was nine and gave me the old one, and I never took it off. It was the first thing I put on in the morning, before my bra, and it hung there all day like a stethoscope. It said *professional*. It said *I'm here to fix you, not to be looked at.*

Here's a thing about being a seamstress. You touch people constantly, and they don't see you at all.

A woman stands in front of the mirror in her slip, and you're on your knees with your hands on her hips and your face six inches from her thigh. She will tell her friend on speakerphone about her affair. She will cry about her father. She will say *God, I'm disgusting*, about a body that is perfectly fine, and she'll say it straight to her reflection, over the top of your head. You are not a person in that room. You're furniture with pins.

I was good at being furniture. I'd been practising since I was fourteen.

I measured thousands of people. Brides, mostly. Bridesmaids, mothers of the bride, prom girls, a man who wanted his late father's suit taken in to fit him for the funeral. He stood very still and cried without making any noise while I chalked the shoulders. I measured all of them, wrote it all down in my mother's little ledger, and never measured myself.

I didn't need to. My mother had done it for me, years ago, without a tape.

My mother died when I was thirty-one. It was her heart, very quickly, in the stockroom, between one box of zippers and the next.

I kept the shop. Of course I kept the shop. I didn't know how to do anything else, and the sign already had my name on it.

I hired nobody. I did the talking now, badly. I had a cat for a while, who left. I had a boyfriend for a while, who left, more or less the same way the cat had: gradually, then all at once, and taking one of my good bowls. I didn't blame either of them. I'd never really given either of them a reason to stay. You don't, when you're furniture.

On the night the world ended, I was thirty-four and finishing a wedding dress.

---

Her name was Dana Pruitt. She was an ER nurse, and she worked nights, so she'd asked whether we could do her final fitting after her shift. Her wedding was in four days. I said yes, because I always said yes, and because I liked her.

I liked her a lot, actually. She was one of the few brides who looked at me when she talked.

She came in at one-forty in the morning in purple scrubs. She was flushed and talking fast, the way people are after twelve hours of other people's emergencies. She had freckles on her forearms and a laugh like somebody kicking a vending machine, and she'd chosen her dress off the rack in about nine minutes. She told me she'd chosen her fiancé in about the same time.

"Kev's a nine-minute man," she said. "In a good way. Don't write that down."

"I'm not writing anything down."

"You're always writing something down." She pointed at the ledger. "You've got my *hips* in there, Katia. You know me better than my mother."

The dress was ivory, sleeveless, with a long train that I'd bustled with eleven little satin loops. It fit her perfectly now, after three fittings. All it needed was the hem. She stood up on the round fitting box in front of the three-way mirror while I knelt in front of her with the pins and the chalk and the tape, the way I'd knelt in front of a thousand women.

"Can I tell you something?" Dana said.

"Sure."

"I don't feel like a bride. I feel like a nurse in a costume." She looked at herself in all three mirrors. "Like if I make one wrong move, someone's going to check my badge."

I didn't say anything. I had pins in my mouth. But I remember thinking, very clearly: *Oh. You too.*

At two-ten she said she was dying for a cigarette. She'd promised Kev she'd quit by the wedding, so this was the last one, absolutely the last, swear to God. She went to step down off the box.

"Not in the dress," I said.

"I'll be careful."

"You'll drag it through a parking lot."

"So come hold it," she said, grinning. "Come on. Be my maid of honour for five minutes. Kev's sister's a nightmare."

So I picked up her train in both arms like a sleeping child, and I followed a bride in a half-pinned wedding dress out into a strip-mall parking lot at two in the morning. I had my tape measure around my neck and a pincushion strapped to my wrist, and my mother's sign was buzzing pink above us.

That's why I'm alive.

I'd like to say it was courage, or instinct. It was the hem.

---

Dana lit her cigarette under the one working light. She took a long drag and blew it straight up, and she said "God, that's good," and I said "you're getting ash on the bodice," and she said "Katia, I love you, shut up."

Then the light changed.

It didn't flicker. Everything in the parking lot lit up from underneath, like the asphalt had become a lampshade. The buzzing from the sign stopped. It didn't trail off. It stopped mid-buzz, like a held breath.

Behind us, Katia's Alterations folded in on itself.

I've tried a hundred times to describe that sound. The closest I've come is a sheet being pulled very quickly off a bed, if the bed were the size of a building. The nail salon went, and the vape shop, and the dry cleaner at the end, all at once and quietly. The roofs dropped straight down into themselves like soufflés. Dust came rolling across the lot toward us in one low, grey wave.

The three-way mirror was in there, and the ledger, and my mother's shears. Thirty-four years of the inside of my life.

Dana's cigarette fell out of her mouth.

There were other people out there in the dark. Not many. A man in a reflective vest by a truck at the gas station across the road. A teenage boy on a bike, standing up on the pedals, frozen. A woman screaming somewhere behind the vape shop, then not screaming any more.

Dana grabbed my wrist, the one with the pincushion on it, and stuck herself on three pins at once. She didn't even notice.

"Kev," she said. "Kev's at home. Kev's *asleep*, Katia."

I didn't say anything. I was looking at the place where the shop had been.

What I felt, and I've never told anybody this, wasn't grief. Not yet. Grief came later, and it took its time and charged by the hour.

What I felt, standing in that dust with a stranger's wedding train still in my arms, was a tiny, horrible, weightless thing. It was like setting down a box you've carried so long you'd forgotten it had a weight.

The three-way mirror was gone.

Nobody was going to look at me in it ever again.

A staircase opened in the middle of the parking lot.

There was no hole and no hatch. Just a clean square of darkness where the faded parking lines had been, with stone steps going down into it, and a soft blue light at the bottom, pleasant, like a hotel pool at night.

The man in the reflective vest was already walking toward it. The boy on the bike was crying. Somebody's car alarm had finally gone off, a full ten seconds late, whooping away into the dust.

> **Notification: A dungeon entrance has opened near you!**
>
> **Crawlers are encouraged to enter promptly. Crawlers who do not enter promptly are encouraged to reconsider. There is a timer. You can't see it. That's part of the fun.**

Dana looked down at herself: the scrubs folded on a fitting chair that no longer existed, the ivory dress, the eleven satin loops.

"I can't go down there in this," she said. Her voice cracked right in the middle. "Katia. I can't go to the end of the world in my *wedding dress*."

I knelt down on the asphalt.

My hands knew what to do before I did. The pincushion was still on my wrist. The tape was still around my neck. I measured her from the knee to the floor, out of habit, and the numbers came out the same as they always had. Then I took the train in both hands and tore it off at the waist seam, all eleven satin loops at once. It ripped clean, because I'd sewn it, and I knew where the weak stitches were.

Dana stared at me.

"There," I said, and pinned the raw edge up so she wouldn't trip. "We can take that in."
