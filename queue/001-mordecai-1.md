People always want to know what I was before. They ask it the same way, too: sideways, like they're asking about a scar. *So what were you, before all this?* As if the answer is going to be a warlord or a prince or some poor bastard who killed a god by accident.

I swept floors.

That's the honest answer. I swept the floor of a potion shop in a town that was mostly fog and fish, on a world that had three moons. All three were too small to be any use, and they hung there like somebody had forgotten to finish them. The shop belonged to a woman named Orrel. She was older than the harbour wall and meaner than the gulls, and she smelled of burnt sugar and copper wire.

I was twelve when I started. She paid me in bread and in lessons I didn't want.

"Heat is a promise," she told me, the first week. She tapped the brass still with one yellow fingernail. "You make it slowly, and you keep it. Break it and the whole batch knows."

I nodded like I understood. I didn't. I thought she was talking about potions.

---

Here's what I learned in that shop. Nobody teaches you this in a dungeon, and it's kept me alive longer than any spell.

Moonroot bruises if you look at it wrong, and a bruised root brews bitter. Grind it with the flat of the pestle, not the edge, and talk to it if you have to. Orrel did. She told the moonroot it was handsome.

A tincture always turns its prettiest colour just before it goes wrong. Gold, usually, or a lovely deep rose. When it goes *right*, it's the colour of weak tea. Nobody ever believes this, and that's why so many people die holding something beautiful.

Customers lie about their symptoms and never about their fears. A man would come in and tell me his chest hurt when he climbed the hill. What he meant was that his father had died at forty-one, and he was forty. You treat the fear first. The chest usually follows.

And the big one: *you don't brew for the potion. You brew for the person who's going to drink it.*

I was nineteen when people in the fog-town started coming to me instead of her. Orrel let them. She pretended she didn't care and drank a little more at night. Once, when she thought I'd gone home, I heard her tell the moonroot that the boy was going to be better than her.

I never told her I heard that. It's one of about four things I regret, and I'm counting carefully.

---

The sky opened on a market day.

I'd like to describe it properly, because the people who watch these things always want the spectacle. But I was in the back room with my hands in a basin of salt water, rinsing seed pods. What I remember is that the light changed. Not brighter. *Thicker*, like the air had been turned into syrup. The salt water went perfectly still. The pods stopped moving.

Orrel was upstairs. I went for the stairs.

The stairs weren't there.

There was a stone room. It smelled of nothing at all, which is the worst smell there is. I didn't know that yet.

There were nine of us in it at first. I'm going to tell you about four, because the other five were gone by the end of the first day, and I never learned their names. I'm not proud of that. In the first hour of a dungeon, you don't learn names. You learn who screams and who goes quiet, and you stand near the quiet ones.

**Dask** was a fisherman. Broad, sunburnt, grey in the beard, built like a mooring post. He'd come out of the harbour with a gutting knife in his hand, and he kept holding it the whole time, like it was a decision he'd already made.

**Pell and Wim** were sisters who ran the bakery on the harbour road. Pell was the older one, tall and sharp and permanently annoyed. Wim was small and round and laughed at things that weren't funny, then apologised for laughing. I'd bought bread from them every morning for seven years. They both looked at me like I was a stranger, because in that room everyone was.

**Hollan** was a soldier from the garrison, off duty, still holding a tin cup of something that steamed. He never put that cup down. Not then and not for a long time after. I think he forgot it was in his hand.

A cheerful voice explained the rules to us. Then it explained them again, more slowly, as if we were the problem.

> **Welcome, Crawlers!**
>
> **You are now participants in the most-watched program in the known galaxy! Each floor of this dungeon contains wonders, treasures and an astonishing variety of things that want to eat you. Find the stairwell before the floor collapses. Do not be on the floor when it collapses. That's really the whole thing!**
>
> **Your viewers are excited to meet you. Try to be interesting. Uninteresting crawlers are a drain on everyone's time.**

"Is that," Wim whispered, "is that a *god?*"

"No," said Hollan. He was the only one of us who'd ever been in a war. "That's management."

---

The dungeon gave us starting gear. Dask got a harpoon, which delighted him. Hollan got a short sword and a dented helmet, which didn't. The sisters got matching cleavers and a debuff called **Flour Allergy (Ironic)**, which I still think was a cheap shot.

I got a satchel, a mortar and pestle, a small brass burner, and a tunic the colour of old porridge.

> **Class Assigned: Hedge Apothecary.**
>
> **You know which plants won't kill you! This is more than most people can say. Your starting kit includes the basic tools of the trade and absolutely nothing that could be described as a weapon.**
>
> **Tip: Many Hedge Apothecaries enjoy a long and fulfilling career standing behind other people!**

"Well," said Pell, looking me up and down. "You're useless."

"I'm useful," I said, "just not yet."

She snorted. Dask laughed, a big ugly harbour laugh, the first laugh anyone had made in that room. Hollan took a sip from his cup.

We walked out into the first floor together, and the five whose names I never learned walked the other way.

---

The first floor was tunnels. Wet ones, which suited Dask and nobody else. We found out what the dungeon thought was funny on the very first turn. It was a creature like a giant louse, with a human face and a very polite voice, and it asked us how our day was going before it tried to take off Wim's arm.

Hollan put his sword through its face. It said "how rude" and died.

> **Achievement Unlocked: First Blood (Shared)!**
>
> **You and your party have killed your first monster! Did it deserve it? Doesn't matter!**
>
> **Reward: A Bronze Adventurer's Box! Please don't open it in the middle of a corridor. Okay, you're going to do that anyway.**

We opened it in the middle of the corridor. It had three healing potions in it, small and stoppered and the colour of a summer sunset.

I didn't like that colour. I couldn't have told you why yet.

On the second day of the first floor, Dask got stung by something with too many legs. It came out of a drain like a hand made of knees. He crushed it with the butt of his harpoon and laughed. Then he stopped laughing and sat down very suddenly on the wet stone.

The skin around the sting was going grey. Grey at the centre, then a ring of yellow, then a fine pink line spreading outward like the edge of a map.

Pell already had a sunset potion in her hand, uncorked. "Here, drink this," she said, "drink it, Dask, *drink it*."

"Wait," I said.

Everybody looked at me. I want you to understand that I had never once in my life told anybody to wait. I was the boy who swept floors. But I had seen that grey and that yellow and that pink line before, on a dock worker who'd been bitten by a harbour eel. The sunset potion would close the wound, and closing the wound would seal the poison in. Orrel had made me watch what happened to the dock worker after a well-meaning sailor stitched him up.

"It'll kill him," I said. "Not right away. Tomorrow."

"You *don't know that*," said Pell.

"No," I said. "But I know what this colour means."

I went down on my knees in the wet and looked at the walls. There were plants growing out of the cracks, because the dungeon is lazy and copies whatever it eats. One of them I knew: a pale creeping thing called deadwife, which grew on the harbour wall. Chewed raw, it'll stop your heart. Steeped slowly, with a little salt, it pulls venom toward the skin like a poultice.

Heat is a promise. You make it slowly.

I lit my little brass burner and I made it slowly. Pell stood over me the entire time and called me every name a baker knows. Bakers know a surprising number of names.

It took eleven minutes. Dask's lips were blue by the end. I packed the steeped leaves into the wound, and the grey crept out of his skin, into the leaves, and turned them black.

He lived.

Dask looked at the blackened leaves for a long time. Then he looked at me. He reached out with one enormous, shaking hand and patted me on the head, the way you'd pat a dog that had pulled you out of the surf.

"Useful," he said.

Pell didn't say anything at all. But that night, when we found the safe room, she put the first bowl of stew in front of me instead of herself. Nobody mentioned it.

After that, nobody asked whether I could fight.
