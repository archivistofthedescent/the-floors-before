Let me tell you about the circuit.

People think a cat show is like a dog show. It isn't. A dog show is grown adults running in circles on a lawn, dragging an animal on a string. A cat show is *court.*

It goes like this. Every weekend there's a different ballroom in a different hotel in a different city. Spokane. Portland. Boise. A place called Puyallup that I refuse to believe is real. The ballroom is full of long tables, and on the tables are rows and rows of cages. They're called benching cages, and every exhibitor decorates their own. Silk curtains, little cushions, fairy lights, signs with your name on in curly gold letters. It's a whole street of tiny palaces, and you sit in yours and receive visitors.

At the front of the room are the rings. That's where the judges are. When it's your turn, a voice calls your number, and your person carries you up in her arms like a gift. She puts you in a cage behind the judge's table, and you wait. Then the judge takes you out, one at a time, and stands you on the table under the lights. They look at your eyes and your ears and the set of your head. Then they lift you up.

That's the part I was born for. The lift.

The judge takes you under the front legs and holds you up high, stretched out long, so the whole room can see the shape of you. For three seconds, everybody in the ballroom is looking at one thing.

And then they put you down, and they write something on a little card, and you go back to your palace and wait to find out if you've won.

I always won.

My palace had lavender curtains, and a cushion shaped like a crown. Miss Beatrice made a sign for it with my name in silver glitter. I thought it was the most beautiful thing in the room, and it was, except me.

The cage on my left belonged to Tamsin Gould.

Tamsin Gould was a woman with very straight hair and a voice like a paper cut. She showed a silver Persian called Lord Bartholomew. He was handsome in the way a good lamp is handsome, and he never once made a sound. Miss Beatrice hated Tamsin Gould more than anybody in the world. You could tell because she was always incredibly nice to her.

"*Tam!* Oh my God, he looks *amazing*, did you do something with his ruff?"

"Just brushing, Bea. Every day. You'd be amazed."

And then, as soon as Tamsin had walked away, Miss Beatrice would take out her phone and type for a very long time with her mouth pressed flat.

I didn't like Lord Bartholomew. He looked at me like I was weather. But I'll admit, he never bit anybody. He never had to. He just sat in the light and let people decide he was perfect.

I never learned how to do that. I'm not sure I'd want to.

---

The cage on my right belonged to Mrs. Hester Kowalczyk, and to Gus.

Gus was a Maine Coon. If you don't know what that is, imagine a cat that a raccoon has described to a bear. He was enormous, brown and black in stripes like a tiger that had been left out in the rain. He had tufts on his ears and a ruff like a lion's, and paws the size of my head. He was very old. Old enough to be grey around his mouth and eyes, and to sleep through most of every show with his chin on the edge of his cushion. Gus did not have a palace. Gus had a plaid blanket and a sign, written in marker on the back of an envelope, that just said GUS.

Mrs. Kowalczyk was old too. She had white hair in a net, and enormous glasses on a beaded chain, and a cardigan with pockets that were always full of things. Tissues. Mints. Little paper cups of chicken. She'd been showing cats, she told everybody, since before most of the judges were born.

And she talked. She talked from the moment she arrived, and she never stopped until she left. She talked to Gus about her grandson, who lived in Phoenix and didn't call. She talked about her knees and the price of gas. She talked about a cat she'd had in 1979 called Mister Fitzwilliam, who had once eaten an entire stick of butter at a show in Tacoma and then won Best in Show anyway.

She talked to me, too. Nobody else at those shows talked *to* me. They talked *about* me. *Look at her coat. Look at that face. Is she the biter?* Mrs. Kowalczyk put her face close to my bars and spoke to me as if I'd asked her a question and she was considering it seriously.

"Well, now," she'd say. "Aren't you having a day."

I didn't understand the words, but I understood that they were for me.

On long show days, when Miss Beatrice was off at the coffee stand, or on her phone, or being nice to Tamsin Gould, I'd lie on my crown cushion with my back to the lavender curtain and listen to Mrs. Kowalczyk talking on the other side. Just talking, the way rain talks on a roof.

That was where I learned to fall asleep to the sound of somebody's voice.

I'm not saying it was important. I'm just saying that's where it started.

There's a routine to a show weekend, and once you've been on the circuit long enough, it becomes a sort of religion.

Thursday is the bath. Two baths, in fact. One with the degreaser, which smells of oranges and lies, and one with the real shampoo. Then the dryer. A Persian coat takes hours to dry properly, and it has to be brushed out the whole time, section by section, so that every hair stands up on its own like a soldier. Miss Beatrice did it on the bathroom floor with a YouTube video playing on her phone, propped against the toilet. She swore the whole way through.

Friday is the drive. The box with the wire door, the passenger seat, the phone calls.

Saturday is court.

Sunday is court again, and then the final. If you've done well enough over the weekend, your number gets called one last time. The judge sets out a row of ten cages and puts the best ten cats in the show in them, one in each. Then they bring you out one at a time, in reverse order, tenth to first, and hang a rosette on each cage. A rosette isn't a ribbon. A ribbon is a strip. A rosette is a ribbon that's been to finishing school: a great frilled circle, with long tails hanging down, and gold writing in the middle.

Every single weekend, for two years, I sat in my lavender palace and listened to other cats' numbers being called for the final.

Lord Bartholomew's was called. Lord Bartholomew's was called a lot.

Mine wasn't. And every Sunday evening, when the final was over and the ballroom was emptying out and people were folding up their curtains, Miss Beatrice packed up my palace without talking. Then she drew the lavender curtain closed over my cage, so I couldn't see out, and she couldn't see in. That was the hardest part. Not the curtain. The *quiet*.

So I learned to get there first.

The minute the final started, before any of the numbers had been called, I would get up off my crown cushion and turn around and sit with my nose to the back of my cage. That way nobody could close me away. I'd already done it. You can't turn somebody away who has already turned away from you. That's just logic.

Then came Spokane.

It was the Midwinter show, in February. The ballroom had chandeliers like upside-down wedding cakes and a carpet the colour of old mustard. There were two hundred and twelve cats in that show. I know the number now. I didn't then.

I'd done well that weekend. I hadn't bitten anybody. I hadn't screamed at a single judge. Mrs. Kowalczyk had brought Gus, and Gus had slept beside me all of Saturday with his chin on his cushion, breathing slowly like something enormous and patient, and somehow that had helped.

On Sunday afternoon the final started, and I did what I always did. I got up and turned around and faced the back of my cage.

And then I heard my number.

I didn't understand words, but I understood numbers, because numbers were how I was called. Mrs. Kowalczyk made a noise on the other side of the curtain like a kettle. Miss Beatrice made no noise at all. Then she opened my door, and lifted me out, and carried me up to the front of the room in her arms like a gift.

The judge was a small round man with a bow tie. He set me in the first cage of the ten. On the far end. Then he called nine more numbers, and Lord Bartholomew was one of them, and so was a cream-coloured Burmese with a face like a disappointed aunt.

Then he took me out first. He held me up in the light, high, stretched long. The whole ballroom went quiet, every chandelier and every person and every cat. It was the longest anybody had ever held me up. I didn't bite him.

He said something, and the room laughed. Then he hung a rosette on my cage. It was purple and silver, as big as a dinner plate, with long tails that hung right down to the table, and there was gold writing on it. I'm telling you exactly what the writing said, because I've been told it so many times since that I know it by heart.

It said BEST IN SHOW.

> **Archive correction:** It said 10TH BEST CAT.

>

>

Miss Beatrice said one word on the drive home.

She said it when we pulled out of the hotel car park, and she said it to nobody, very quietly, the way you'd say the name of something you'd dropped down a drain.

"*Tenth.*"

That was all. It was a down word. I didn't know what it meant, but I knew it went all the way down.

The rosette wasn't in the car. She'd left it on the folding table in the ballroom with the empty cages.

I know what happened to it, because Mrs. Kowalczyk told me. Not then. I mean, she told me *then*, but I understood her later. She'd picked it up off the table and brought it out to the car park after us, and caught Miss Beatrice at the car. She tucked it through the wire door of my box, and it lay there beside me all the way home, purple and silver, tails curling round my paws.

"You hang on to that," Mrs. Kowalczyk said, through the wire. "Well, now. You hang on to that."

I hung on to it.

---

She came to two more shows after Spokane. The second one, she came alone.

There was no Gus. There was the plaid blanket, folded on the table in front of her, and the sign on the back of the envelope, and no cat behind it. She sat beside my palace all day long with her hands in her cardigan pockets. For the first time since I'd known her, she didn't say very much at all.

Late in the afternoon, she put her face down close to my bars, and her big glasses slid down her nose on their beaded chain.

"He was very old, sweetheart," she said. "He was very, very old, and he had a good long time. That's all anybody gets, if they're lucky."

Then she was quiet for a while. Then, very softly, she started talking again. About Phoenix, and her grandson. About her knees. About a stick of butter in Tacoma in 1979.

I turned around and faced the curtain, and lay down, and fell asleep to it.

She didn't come to the next show, or the one after that. I looked for her at every show for the rest of that season. I'm a princess. I didn't *look*. I *surveyed*. I surveyed for her.
