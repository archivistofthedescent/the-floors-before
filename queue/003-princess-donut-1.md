I was born a princess. I want to be very clear that this is not a figure of speech, the way humans say it about a little girl in a plastic tiara at a birthday party. I mean it literally. I come from a line of queens.

My great-great-grandmother on my mother's side sat in the lap of a duchess. I don't know which duchess. It doesn't matter. When you sit in the lap of a duchess, the duchess is the one who should be remembering it. Before her there were cats who lived in palaces in Persia, which is where the name comes from, obviously. Cats who were fed from silver dishes by men in very tall hats. Cats who had their own servants and their own rooms and, I assume, their own small thrones.

I was born into that, is what I'm telling you. I was born into a *legacy*.

I opened my eyes for the first time on a bed of the softest cloth in the world. It was warm, and it hummed, and the whole room smelled faintly of flowers.

> **Archive correction:** The subject was born in the laundry room of a split-level ranch house eleven miles outside Yakima, Washington. The bed of the softest cloth in the world was a pile of towels on top of a running dryer. The flowers were a fabric softener sheet called *Spring Meadow Burst.* To her left was a plastic tub labelled XMAS LIGHTS (TANGLED). To her right was a water heater that made a noise like a man clearing his throat.
>
> I've checked the duchess, by the way. There was no duchess. There was a woman in Boise named Duchess Cheryl who ran a cat-grooming business. Your great-great-grandmother sat in her lap for a photograph. It's in a scrapbook.
>
> It's a nice photograph. I'll give you that.

---

There were five of us in the litter. I was the most beautiful. That's not boasting, it's just a fact, and I'd ask you to accept it the way you accept that water is wet or that Carl is going to make a bad decision before lunch.

My nursemaid was a woman named Darlene.

Darlene was a person of simple means. She wore the same green sweatshirt every day, and it said STATE CHAMPS on the front, which I believe referred to a sports team and not to us. She had large soft hands that smelled of tuna and hand cream. She talked constantly, mostly to the radio, which was always on and always playing a man who was angry about the weather. She knew she was in the presence of greatness. I could tell by the way she held me. Other kittens, she scooped. Me, she lifted.

It was Darlene who gave me my name.

I slept curled in a perfect circle, nose to tail, and she looked down at me one morning and said "well, look at you, you little donut." There are people who will tell you that's a silly reason to name somebody. Those people don't understand symbolism. A donut is a circle. You know what else is a circle?

A crown.

I'm not going to explain this further. If you don't see it, you don't see it.

---

My brothers and sisters left one at a time.

People came to the house in cars. They sat on Darlene's flowered couch with their knees together and drank weak coffee out of mugs that said things like WORLD'S OKAYEST GRANDMA. Then they picked us up, one after another, and turned us over and looked at our faces very closely. They looked at our faces in a way I found rude even then. Then they wrote Darlene a cheque and carried one of us out to the car in a little plastic box with a wire door.

First my brother with the orange smudge. Then my two sisters, together, to a family with a little boy who cried when he saw them. Then my last brother, the big one, who went to a man from Portland who wanted "something to breed off."

I was saved for last.

I understood exactly why. Darlene couldn't bear to part with me. You don't put the crown jewels in the front window. You keep them in the back, in the safe, and you only bring them out for someone who deserves them.

I want to tell you about the day Miss Beatrice came, because it was the most important day of my life until it wasn't.

It was raining. It's always raining in my memories of that house. The kind of rain that doesn't fall so much as hang around. I heard a car pull into the driveway, and the screen door bang, and then a voice I'd never heard before, high and quick and laughing.

"Oh my *God*, Darlene, it's like a hoarder's house but for cats. I mean that as a compliment."

And then she was in the laundry room doorway, and I saw her for the first time.

She was young, with dark hair pulled up very high and a pair of sunglasses perched on top of her head, even though it was raining. She smelled of vanilla and car air freshener and something underneath that I didn't have a word for yet. I've thought about it since. The word I'd use now is *ambition.* She had long fingernails, painted a colour like the inside of a seashell, and she was carrying her phone in her hand the way other people carry a cup of coffee, like she'd feel naked without it.

She crouched down in front of the dryer and looked at me.

I looked back.

Everyone else who'd come to that house had looked at my face as though they were checking it for mistakes. Miss Beatrice didn't do that. She tilted her head and looked at me the way you look at something in a shop window you know you can't afford. I knew in that moment that she had come for me and only me, that she'd driven through the rain from the city because she'd heard there was a princess in a laundry room, and that she'd known me before she ever saw me.

"Hi, gorgeous," she said. "Are you going to win me things?"

I didn't know what *win* meant yet. I knew it was a question. I knew the answer was yes.

I rode to the city in a plastic box with a wire door, on the passenger seat of Miss Beatrice's little car. I screamed the entire way. I was not frightened. I was making announcements.

Miss Beatrice talked on her phone with one hand and drove with the other, and every so often she put a seashell fingernail through the wire and touched my nose.

"No, she's *gorgeous*," she was saying. "She's the one. I can feel it. Mom, I'm not wasting money, this is an *investment.*"

I didn't understand words then. I want to be clear about that, because some of you are going to ask how I can remember what people said before I could understand them. The answer is that I remember the *music.* I remember the way her voice went up on *gorgeous* and down on *investment.* Up was for me. Down was for something else.

I decided, on that drive, to only listen to the up parts.

---

Miss Beatrice lived in an apartment with white walls, white carpet and a white couch nobody was allowed to sit on. I fitted in perfectly. I believe she had decorated it for me in advance.

The very first night, she gave me a bath.

I'd never had a bath. I'd never even considered a bath. A bath, it turns out, is when someone you trust holds you down in hot water and rubs soap into you while you explain, at the top of your voice, that you are going to kill them. Then they wrap you in a towel and hold a machine to you that roars and blows hot air into your face for an hour and a half.

When it was over, Miss Beatrice set me on the white couch, the one nobody was allowed to sit on, and stepped back and took a picture of me with her phone.

Then she took forty-one more.

"Oh, *look* at you," she whispered. "Oh, they're going to *die.*"

I didn't know who *they* were, but I was pleased that they were going to die. It sounded like a compliment. And when she turned the phone round to show me the pictures, I saw myself for the first time. Not in a puddle or a window, but properly. White and enormous and furious, with a face like a full moon. My fur stood out all around me like a cloud that had decided to be a lion.

I thought: *Oh. There I am.*

It was Miss Beatrice who completed my name. The registry, which is the government of cats, needed a full name for its records. She sat on the floor with her laptop on the coffee table, and me in her lap, and she said "Donut" out loud a few times, the way you'd test whether a step was going to hold your weight.

"Donut," she said. "Donut. Ugh. It sounds like a cat that lives in a garage."

She thought for a long time. Then she typed something, and smiled, and kissed the top of my head.

"*Princess* Donut," she said.

I want you to understand what happened in that moment. Darlene gave me a name, and it was a good name, but it was a name for a laundry room. Miss Beatrice gave me back my *title.* The one that had been mine all along, passed down from Persia and the silver dishes and the men in tall hats. She didn't invent it. She recognised it. That's different.

I loved her very much from that moment. I want that on the record too, before we go any further. Whatever else I'm going to tell you, and whatever else *it* is going to tell you, because I can feel it waiting to butt in, I loved her.

---

My first show was six weeks later, in a hotel ballroom by the airport. It had a carpet the colour of a bruise.

I'll tell you about it properly later, because the circuit deserves its own part. This is just to say that I was the youngest and the most beautiful cat in my class. The judge was a tall man with a moustache. He lifted me up to the lights in both hands and held me there, and the whole room went quiet. That was the first time I understood that people could go quiet *for* me. Not because I'd screamed at them. Just because I was there.

Then I bit him.

I don't regret it. He'd held me up long enough. You don't keep a princess in the air indefinitely.

On the drive home, Miss Beatrice didn't touch my nose through the wire. She talked on her phone again, and there were no up parts at all. Only down, and down, and down.

I didn't understand words then.

I understood that the car was dark, and that the rain was back, and that the only voice in there was going down. So I turned around in my little box, as far as it would let me, until I was looking at the back of it. A plastic wall, with a sticker on it that said THIS SIDE UP.

I faced it all the way home.

It was the first time I did that. It wasn't the last.
