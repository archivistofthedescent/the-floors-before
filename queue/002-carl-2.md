> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 4 DAYS.**

The binder had a tab called GROOMING, and the tab had a schedule, and the schedule had a column labelled *Daily* that was much longer than I thought a column should be.

Eyes wiped. Face folds checked. Undercoat combed, wide tooth first, then fine. Nails inspected for "attitude." Bea had actually written that. *Attitude.* Underlined twice.

I'd been skipping most of it. Not on purpose. I'd just assumed a cat was a cat, and that cats handled themselves, and that the column was more of a suggestion. Then on the fourth day before the end of the world I came out of the shower and found Princess Donut sitting in the middle of the living room floor with a clump of her own fur in front of her. She'd coughed it up. She was looking at it, and then at me, and then at it again, like a lawyer laying out evidence.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. I hear you."

I got the combs out of the little zip case. Wide tooth first. I sat on the floor with my back against the couch, and I put her on a towel across my knees. She went rigid as a loaf of bread left out overnight, and I started.

It took forty minutes. You'd think it would take five. It does not take five. There is more cat under the top layer of a Persian than you would believe, a whole hidden second cat, and all of it is tangled.

Somewhere around minute twenty she stopped being rigid.

Somewhere around minute thirty she started making a noise. It wasn't purring. It was lower than purring, like a small engine idling somewhere far away. I knew that sound. I'd spent years listening to engines. That's the sound of something that's been running rough for a long time, finally getting the fuel mix right.

"There you go," I said. "There she is."

She bit me when I got to the back legs. Not hard. More of a reminder about boundaries.

I was late for work. Lyle didn't say anything. He just looked at the cat hair on my jacket, all over it, an absurd quantity, like I'd wrestled a carpet. He nodded slowly, the way he'd nodded when I told him about the cat in the first place.

"She coughed up a hairball," I said.

"They do that."

"I'm on the schedule now. There's a column."

"Sure there is." He tossed me a rag. "Slip twelve. The Bayliner. Guy says it's making a noise like a goose."

It wasn't making a noise like a goose. It was making a noise like a seized water pump impeller, which, to be fair, does sound a little like a goose, if you've never heard either. I spent the morning on my back in an engine compartment that smelled of diesel and old sandwiches. Every time I moved, a little more white fur drifted down off my sleeves and landed in the bilge.

At lunch I sat on an upturned bucket at the end of the dock and called Bea.

It rang four times. Then her voice came on, bright and recorded, from some day when she'd been in a good mood. *Hey, it's Bea, you know what to do.*

I did know what to do. I'd done it before. The tone came, and the line was open, and the harbour slapped against the pilings under me while I tried to work out what to say.

*She's fine. She coughed up a hairball. I'm doing the column. Are you coming back. Are we still.*

I hung up without saying anything.

That's three, I thought. Three calls, three no-messages. I told myself it was because I didn't want to sound needy. That's not true. I didn't leave a message because if I left one, she'd have to decide whether to answer it. And as long as I didn't, she didn't have to decide, and I didn't have to find out.

I know how that sounds. I knew how it sounded then. I ate my sandwich anyway.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 3 DAYS.**

On the third day before the end of the world, Mrs. Okafor caught me on the stairs.

She was standing in the doorway of the ground-floor apartment in a quilted housecoat the colour of a ripe plum. She was holding a casserole dish wrapped in a towel, and her face said she'd been waiting. Mrs. Okafor was in her seventies. She'd lived in the building since before it had working buzzers. She considered the mailman, the landlord and the city of Seattle's recycling policy her personal enemies, in roughly that order.

"You," she said. "The cat man."

"Hi, Mrs. Okafor."

"That cat cries."

I stopped on the stair. "She what?"

"When you go out in the morning. She sits at your door and cries. I hear it through the ceiling, through the pipes. Every day, an hour, more." She pushed the casserole dish into my hands. It was still hot. "You leave a radio on for her. Talk radio. Not music. She wants a voice."

"How do you know she wants a voice?"

Mrs. Okafor looked at me the way the cat did, like I was an appliance with a fault.

"Because I am old and I live alone," she said, "and I leave the radio on."

Then she shut the door.

I stood there for a while holding a hot dish of what turned out to be the best chicken and rice I've ever eaten. I was thinking about a cat sitting at a door crying for an hour, and about the fact that she'd never once done it while I was in the apartment. When I was home she ignored me with real dedication. Apparently she saved the rest for when I couldn't hear it.

I'd known some people like that. I'd been raised by one.

---

That night I set up the radio. It was an old clock radio, a cheap grey one with fake wood on the front. I found a station where two men argued about baseball trades in a way that never ended, and I put it on the shelf by the door, low.

Donut watched me do this from the arm of the couch. When I turned it on she flattened her ears like the radio had insulted her mother. She ignored it for a whole minute, very loudly.

Then she got down, walked over, and lay down underneath it.

I sat at the kitchen table with the binder open. I wasn't reading it. I was just looking at the red pen in the corner. *Carl is good with her. Better than me.*

And I was thinking about my mother, which I don't do much. I want to be clear about that. I don't sit around thinking about my mother. I'm not that guy. But the radio was going, two men disagreeing at a murmur, and the cat was lying under it, and I couldn't stop.

Here's the thing about my father. I'm going to say it once and fast, because that's the only way I can say it.

He drank. He drank the way some people go to work, on a schedule, with a lunch break. When he drank he got loud, and when he got loud he broke things, and sometimes the things were things and sometimes they weren't. There. That's it. That's all you get.

My mother had a trick.

On the bad nights she'd come into my room and sit on the end of my bed in the dark. She'd talk, quietly and steadily, never stopping, about anything at all. The grocery list. A woman at her work who wore too much perfume. The plot of a TV show she'd seen. Whether you could grow tomatoes on a fire escape. She'd go on and on in this low, flat voice. She never raised it and she never paused long enough for the noise from the kitchen to get in.

I didn't understand what she was doing until much later. She wasn't telling me stories. She was covering the sound. She was laying her voice down over the top of his like a blanket over a broken window, so that I'd have something else to listen to.

She'd keep going until I fell asleep. Every time. However long it took.

I never once heard her stop. I don't know how long she sat there after I was out. I don't know if she went back to the kitchen, or what was waiting for her when she did. I've thought about it more than I'd like.

The cat was still under the radio.

I didn't decide to do it. I just got up from the table, crossed the room and sat down on the floor next to the shelf, with my back against the wall. After a second I switched the radio off.

Donut lifted her head and glared at me. *I was listening to that.*

"Yeah," I said. "I know. Sorry. Here."

And I talked.

I told her about the Bayliner, and the impeller, and the goose. I told her the plot of a movie I'd half-watched on a plane once, about a guy who lives in an airport, and I got most of it wrong. I told her Mrs. Okafor thought she was a crier, and that I'd keep that between us. I told her the tide tables for the south side, because I knew them, and because they're very calming if you say them slowly. High at four-twelve. Low at ten-forty.

I kept my voice low and flat. I didn't stop.

It wasn't for the cat, not really. Or not only. It was for whatever was on the other side of the door. Nothing, it turned out. Just the stairwell and Mrs. Okafor's radio coming faintly up through the pipes. But I'd learned a long time ago that you don't wait to find out what's on the other side of the door. You just keep talking until the person next to you is asleep.

She fell asleep around high tide, in my telling. Low at ten-forty, high at four-twelve. Her head was on my sock.

I stayed there on the floor afterwards for a long time. I didn't want to move my foot.

Later, in bed, I picked up my phone.

The screen lit the ceiling blue. I opened my messages, found Bea's name, and typed: *She's okay. She misses you.*

I looked at it for a long time.

Then I deleted *She misses you*, because I didn't know if it was true. You can't put something on a cat that you're not sure about. That's not fair to the cat.

I sent *She's okay.*

It said *Delivered.* It never said *Read.*

Down in the stairwell, two floors below, Mrs. Okafor's radio was still going. You could just about make it out if you lay very still. A man's voice, low and steady, saying something about the weather. He didn't stop.

I lay very still.
