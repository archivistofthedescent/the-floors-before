> **ARCHIVE ACCESS EXTENDED. File 001, appendix.**
>
> I know. I said "END OF FILE." I meant it at the time. Then Production noticed the completion rate on this one and asked very nicely whether there might be any more. There is always more, Archivist. That's the second rule of this business. The first rule is that you don't get to stop watching just because you've seen enough.

---

The first thing they give you, when you sign, is a room.

Mine was on the third floor that first season. It had a stone floor, a long wooden counter, a brass still that wasn't Orrel's but had clearly been copied from somebody's memory of it, and a shelf. Just one shelf, empty, running the whole length of the back wall. I stood in front of it for a long time. I think the dungeon wanted to see what I'd do.

What I did was ask for the cup.

You get one personal item. That's in the contract, page twelve, a clause nobody reads because it sits in the middle of four pages about liability for spontaneous combustion. *One item of sentimental value, recovered from salvage, subject to availability.* Everything that falls through a collapsing floor goes somewhere. Nothing in this place is ever really thrown away. It's just filed.

> **Personal Item Requested: Tin Cup (Dented).**
>
> **Item Value: 0 gold. Item Properties: None. Item Condition: Bad.**
>
> **Are you sure? You could have had a sword. Guides aren't allowed to use swords, but you could have *had* one.**

It arrived on the counter with a small, embarrassed clink. There was still a brown ring dried in the bottom of it, from nine floors and a soldier's wife.

I didn't wash it. I put it on the shelf. First thing on there.

Stock on a shelf.

---

Here are the rules for a guide. They're engraved on a brass plate by the door, in case you forget, and you can't forget, because they're also engraved on the inside of your head.

A guide does not leave the room.

A guide does not fight. If a guide raises a hand against a monster, the hand stops working. If a guide raises a hand against a crawler, so does everything else.

A guide teaches. A guide may advise, demonstrate, brew, scold, and pour drinks. A guide may not tell a crawler what is behind any particular door. There's a list of the things a guide is forbidden to say. It's long, and whenever you get near one, your tongue goes thick and stupid in your mouth, like you've been stung.

And a guide does not go down the stairs. When the floor below opens, your crawlers walk out of your room and they don't come back. Not ever. Not even the ones who live.

*Especially* not the ones who live.

I read the rules once, the first day, and then I read the plate every morning for the next forty seasons, because a rule's a kind of jar too. If you don't know what's in it, you don't have it.

---

My first crawler was a seamstress called Ysolde.

She came from a world I won't name, because it's gone, and naming it doesn't bring it back. It just gives Legal something to do. She had six fingers on each hand and a needle she'd carried in her teeth the whole way down to the third floor. She sat at my counter and looked at me as if I was a stranger, and I was one.

I gave her the whole speech. I told her about weak tea. I told her about the gold and the rose and the beautiful sunset colours, and how a thing always looks its loveliest just before it goes wrong. I told her heat is a promise. I told her that you brew for the person, not the potion. I made her a cave pig tonic for the ache in her hands and warned her about the blue.

She didn't listen. She turned blue.

She laughed.

I sat in that room after she'd gone down the stairs, with her blue laugh still somewhere in the air, and I felt something I hadn't felt since the fog-town. I'd been *good* at something, and someone had needed it.

> **Crawler Ysolde [REDACTED] has died.**
>
> **Floor: 5. Cause of death: She found a beautiful potion in a Gold Box. It was rose-coloured. She drank it. Somewhere, a guide is not surprised.**

I heard about it two days later. Guides get a notice. It's a courtesy. I've always thought it was the cruellest courtesy anyone ever designed.

---

So I made a list. Of course I did. I'd always made lists.

Ysolde. The Brannick twins, who argued about everything, including which of them was older, and who died on the fourth floor arguing about which way to run. A priest named Ammet who prayed to a god his planet didn't have any more and asked me, very politely, whether I thought it was still listening. I told him I didn't know. He said that was the most honest answer anyone had given him since the sky opened, and he thanked me for it, and he left a little carved bead on my counter that I still have. A lizard woman called Gresh who made it to the ninth floor, two short of where I'd made it, and sent me nothing at all, because crawlers can't send things up. I liked to think she would have.

A boy called Fen.

Fen was nineteen. He swept the floor of my room without being asked, the first night, because he didn't know what else to do with his hands. I watched him do it and had to go and stand behind the still for a while.

I taught Fen everything. Not just the speech. The real things. How to grind moonroot with the flat of the pestle. How to talk to it, if you had to. How to take stock of your party every morning, even when they hated you for it. *Especially* when they hated you for it.

He listened. He was the only one who ever listened about the blue. He drank the cave pig tonic anyway, because his knee was bad, and he walked out of my room blue to his toenails with a grin on his face, and I thought, *this one.* This one's going to go through.

> **Crawler Fen [REDACTED] has died.**
>
> **Floor: 6. Cause of death: He stopped to help a party member who had been stung. He knew exactly which wall-weed to use. He did not have eleven minutes.**
>
> **Viewer comment, highlighted: "lol the guide's apprentice."**

That's when I stopped learning names.

Not at once. It's not a decision, it's a drift. You learn a name, then you learn it a little less hard the next season. Then you catch yourself calling someone *the tall one* or *the one with the axe*, and they don't correct you, because they'll be dead in a week and they know it.

I went back to the first hour of the first day. I learned who screamed and who went quiet, and I stood near the quiet ones, and I kept my hands busy.

---

That was also when I started putting something other than tea in the cup.

I'm not going to make a joke about this. People always expect me to, and I've got plenty, but not here. The dungeon sells a liquor on the early floors that's the colour of honey in sunlight. It's genuinely the most beautiful thing you've ever seen in a glass. Gold, Archivist, the exact gold of a batch about to turn. I knew that. I'm the one who taught the speech.

I drank it out of Hollan's tin cup anyway.

> **Status Effect Applied: Functional.**
>
> **Duration: Ongoing. Effects: The subject continues to perform all assigned duties to a satisfactory standard. The subject's hands are steady from approximately the second drink onward. The subject has stopped being upset about it, which Production considers a real growth moment.**

The dungeon didn't mind. Why would it? A guide who drinks is still useful. Sometimes he's more useful. He's funnier with the crawlers. He gives the speech with a bit of swagger. He makes the speech into a *bit*, and the viewers love a bit.

Seasons went by. The dungeon changed my face more than once. It likes its staff fresh, and it was always very interested in what I'd look like with more teeth, or fewer, or a tail. I woke up as something new at the start of one season and spent the first morning learning how to hold a pestle all over again. I've never decided whether that was a punishment or a promotion.

They moved my room as well. Third floor, then second, then right up near the top of it, where the crawlers are still in shock and still have the dust of their own houses in their hair. It's better that way, Production said. Get them early. Get them before they've learned anything the wrong way.

I didn't argue. Arguing isn't useful.

Every morning I took stock. Room: clean. Still: working. Shelf: full. Guide: functional.

I never put myself on the shelf. I promised Wim I would, once. I've told you I'm a liar.

---

I want to tell you about one thing that happened somewhere around the thirtieth season, because I've never told anyone, and the Archive has probably lost the footage anyway.

There was a crawler, a big man, a fisherman as it happens. They're common. Most worlds have water, and most water has someone who goes out on it before dawn. He came into my room with a hook in his hand like it was a decision he'd already made. He was sunburnt and grey in the beard, and he laughed at something I said, a big ugly harbour laugh. I dropped the cup.

It didn't break. It's tin. It bounced, and rolled under the counter, and the gold went everywhere on the stone.

I got down on my knees to find it. I stayed down there for longer than I needed to. The fisherman, who was not Dask and did not know anything about Dask, crouched down on the other side of the counter and asked me if I was all right.

I told him I was fine. I told him heat is a promise.

He died on the fourth floor, in a corridor of doors.

Not the Gauntlet. A different one. The dungeon recycles. But I got the notice, and I read it, and I put the cup on the shelf and didn't drink out of it for a whole season.

Then I did again. It's a long time, forty seasons.

> **Achievement Unlocked: Long Service!**
>
> **You have completed forty seasons as a Guide without being reassigned, decommissioned, or rendered into component parts! Your former crawlers would be very proud, if any of them were still in a position to be anything!**
>
> **Reward: A plaque. It is identical to the brass plate by your door, but slightly smaller. You may hang it next to the first one.**

I hung it upside down. Nobody's ever said anything.

---

The notice for this season arrived the way it always does. A chime, a line of glowing text above the counter, and the faint smell of nothing coming through the walls as the floor finished assembling itself around me.

> **Incoming Season Briefing.**
>
> **Planet: Earth. Status: Collapsed. Crawler count: Large. Viewer interest: Already very high.**
>
> **Your assignment: one (1) human male, adult. One (1) cat, domestic.**
>
> **Note from me:** I want to say for the record that I did not choose the cat. I want to say that, Archivist, and I want you to think about how often I say things for the record.

I read it twice. Then I laughed, and it came out of me like something breaking loose, and I had to sit down.

A cat.

Forty-odd seasons. Seamstresses and soldiers and priests and lizard women and a boy who swept floors. And now they were sending me a man and his cat.

I stopped laughing, eventually. The room was very quiet. It smelled of nothing.

I took stock.

Room: clean, more or less. Still: working. Shelf: full. Forty-odd seasons of jars, all labelled in a hand that's changed shape three times, and every one of them the colour of weak tea. Plaque: upside down. Guide: functional. Hands: steady, but only from about the second drink.

And on the counter, in front of me, Hollan's tin cup, half full of honey-gold.

I looked at that gold for a long time.

A tincture always turns its prettiest colour just before it goes wrong. That's the first thing I ever learned that was worth knowing, and I've told it to more people than I can count, and I have counted. I've told it to all of them except me.

I poured it out.

It went across the stone in a long beautiful stripe, the way it had the day I dropped the cup. Then I lit the little burner under the still, and waited for the water, and I made it slowly. Orrel's way. A pinch of something from the third jar along, not too much, and then a bit less than that.

Weak. She always made it too weak, Hollan said. He used to complain.

I filled the cup. It was the right colour. It's always the right colour, when it's right.

Somewhere out in the corridor there were footsteps. Two sets. One heavy, in boots, a man's tread, uneven, like he'd been walking far longer than he'd planned to. The other was light and quick, little claws ticking on the stone, and it was going a good deal faster than the boots, as if it didn't care whether it was followed. There was a voice, too, low and exasperated, saying something I couldn't make out. Whatever the cat said back, it sounded like an opinion.

I put the cup down on the counter.

I didn't pick it up again.

The handle of the door began to turn.
