> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 19 HOURS.**

The cobbler had the boots on the counter before I got the door shut behind me.

He didn't say hello. He'd never said hello. He just turned them round so the toes pointed at me, the way you'd present a dish in a fancy restaurant, and waited.

They looked like my boots, and they didn't. The leather was the same leather, every crease and scuff and the long pale scar across the right toe where I'd caught it on a deck cleat. But it had been cleaned and fed and polished until it went deep and dark, like water at night. The soles were new, thick and black and stitched all the way round in tight even loops. The laces matched. That was the thing that got me, stupidly. For the first time in years, both laces were the same.

"New welt," he said. "New heel. I kept the insoles. They had your foot in them already. You don't throw that away."

I picked up the left one. I bent it at the toe, where it had been talking all month.

It didn't say anything.

"How much?"

He named a number. It was less than it should have been, and I told him so, and he looked at me over both pairs of glasses like I'd said something tasteless at a funeral.

So I paid what he said. He took the money, and then he reached under the counter and put a small round tin of black polish next to the boots. He put it down with a little click.

"Every month," he said. "Not when they look like they need it. Before."

"Before they look like they need it."

"By the time they look like it, it's late." He tapped the tin. "Everything is like this."

I sat down on the bench by his window, took off my sneakers and put the boots on. I laced them up, left then right, double knot, the way I'd been taught in boot camp by a petty officer who'd made us do it in the dark. I stood up.

The cobbler watched me walk to the door and back. Heel, toe. Heel, toe. No flap.

"Better," he said. He turned back to his bench and picked up his tools. Then, without looking at me: "You're still carrying it. But now you're carrying it right."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I said thanks. He'd already stopped listening.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 14 HOURS.**

The marina was quiet. The rain had blown through overnight and left everything rinsed and cold. The water in the slips was flat and grey, with a skin of ice in the corners where the sun didn't get. It was colder than it had any right to be. My breath came out in clouds.

Lyle looked at my feet for a long time.

"Huh," he said.

"Told you they were worth fixing."

"You did." He handed me a coffee. It still tasted like the bilge. "You look less like hell."

"Thanks, Lyle."

"I mean it. You look like a guy who slept." He squinted out at the water. "Cat warming up to you?"

I thought about it. About the towel and the comb and the idling-engine noise. About her head on my sock.

"She tolerates me," I said.

"That's how it starts." He slapped the doorframe twice, the way he did when he was finished with a conversation. "See you tomorrow, Carl."

"See you tomorrow."

I rebuilt a lower unit, replaced a bilge pump and told a dentist that his boat was fine and he just had to stop running it out of fuel. At five I put my tools away in the order I always put them away, and I walked home in my good boots. Heel, toe. Heel, toe. I'm not saying I was happy. But I noticed my own walking for the first time in a month, and it sounded right.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 10 HOURS.**

I knocked on Mrs. Okafor's door to give the casserole dish back. I'd washed it twice.

She opened it in the plum housecoat, took the dish and looked down at my boots.

"Good," she said. It was the first time I'd ever heard her use the word.

"The radio's working," I said. "I think. Thanks for that."

"I know it's working. I didn't hear her today." She paused in the doorway. Behind her, her own radio was murmuring away, somebody reading the news in a low voice. "You talk to her yourself, too?"

"Sometimes."

"Good," she said again. Two in one day. Then she shut the door.

Upstairs, Donut was sitting in her spot.

It was a new spot. A couple of nights earlier, I'd found something in the back pocket of the binder, a little rosette, and I'd pinned it on the wall by the bookshelf at cat height. That's where she went to sit now when she was in a mood. Facing it, with her back to me, very straight, the way she used to face the bare wall when I put the wrong food in her bowl.

She was facing it now. It hadn't been a good day. The heat in the building kept clanking on and off, and the clouds out the window were low and orange with city light, and the air felt heavy. Wrong somehow. Like the inside of an engine just before it throws a rod.

"Hey," I said. "Hey, Princess."

She didn't turn round. But her tail moved once.

---

I sat at the kitchen table with the binder and a blue ballpoint pen.

I'd been thinking about this all day, ever since the cobbler said *before.* Not when they look like they need it. Before.

I turned to the very back page, where Bea's notes were. Different pens, different years. *Blue blanket, not grey. Afraid of the vacuum. Sleeps better if someone talks to her.* And in the corner, small, in red: *Carl is good with her. Better than me.*

Under that I wrote, in blue, in handwriting that looked nothing like hers:

*Donut is afraid of being left. Will NEVER admit it. Do not leave her. —C.*

I looked at it for a while. It looked like a note somebody would write if they were planning to leave, so that the next person would know. I almost crossed it out.

I didn't. I wasn't planning to go anywhere. I was writing it down for whoever came next, because that's what the binder was for. Everyone who ever looked after her had written down what they'd learned. Now I had too.

When I looked up, she was watching me from across the room. I don't know how long she'd been doing it.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 4 HOURS.**

I talked her to sleep that night like I always did, sitting on the floor with my back against the couch. She lay on the towel across my knees, even though I wasn't combing her, which was new.

And this time I told her everything.

I hadn't planned to. I'd planned on tide tables. But I'd got as far as *low at ten-forty*, and then I heard myself telling her about the crab boat. The strobes going up and down in the water. The collar in my fist, and four minutes, and the line going slack. Then my mother on the end of my bed in the dark, talking about tomatoes on fire escapes. The funeral. Three empty chairs. *Those are good boots.*

I said it all in the low flat voice. I didn't stop. I'd never said any of it out loud before, not to the Coast Guard counsellor, not to Bea and not to Lyle. And it turns out that when you say a thing that size in a voice that steady, to a cat, it doesn't come out like a confession. It comes out like the weather. It comes out like something that's simply true, and has been, and will be tomorrow too.

"So that's the rule," I told her. "You don't let go of the one who's in front of you. And right now that's you. Sorry. I know you'd have preferred someone with a pedigree."

Her eyes were closed. The idling-engine noise was going, very low.

I put her on the bed. I put my boots by the bed, too, instead of by the door, laces loose and tongues pulled out the way the petty officer taught us, so that you can get into them in the dark. I don't know why I did that. I hadn't done it since the cutter.

Maybe it was the air. It felt like a storm coming.

I fell asleep with my hand on her back.

> **TIME UNTIL PLANETARY COLLAPSE: 0 HOURS. 9 MINUTES.**

> **Archivist, I'm going to stop talking now. I want you to hear this bit with nothing on top of it.**

Cold woke me up.

Not a sound. Cold, a long slow ribbon of it running across the bed from the direction of the hall. And under my hand, where there should have been a cat, there was a warm dent in the duvet.

I was up before I was awake. "Donut?"

The apartment door was open. Not wide, about a cat's width. I've had a long time to think about how that happened, and I've come up with a lot of theories, and none of them matter. The door was open and the stairwell light was buzzing, and down below, very faintly, Mrs. Okafor's radio was still going.

And from further down, from the street door, I heard a sound I'd heard every day that week through the ceiling. I'd only been told about it before. It was the sound of a cat crying. She was out, and she was scared, and she'd never admit it.

I was in my boxers. I didn't think about that. Later, I'd have a lot of time to think about that too.

I shoved my bare feet into the boots, which were right where I'd left them by the bed, laces loose, the way you do it in the dark. I grabbed my jacket off the hook on the way past. I hit the stairs three at a time. The new soles didn't slip once. Not once. The cobbler had done good work.

I went past Mrs. Okafor's door, past a man's voice reading the weather in a low steady murmur, and I shouldered the street door open, and the cold hit me like the sea coming over the rail.

The street was orange and empty. There was frost on the parked cars and the air had that heavy, wrong feeling, worse now, like the whole sky was holding its breath. And there she was, out in the middle of the road, a pale puff of fur in the streetlight. Her back was to me, very straight, facing something I couldn't see.

"Donut," I said. "Hey. Hey, Princess. It's me. I'm right here."

I kept my voice low and flat. I kept walking toward her. Heel, toe. I didn't stop talking.

I was going to get her. I was not going to let go.
