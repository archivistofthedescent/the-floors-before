There were four of us who went down the stairs in that parking lot, and for a while that was a family.

Dana, in her shortened wedding dress, with the bodice still pinned at the back because I hadn't had time to finish it. The boy on the bike, whose name was Toby. He was sixteen, skinny as a rake, and he'd been riding home from a friend's house at two in the morning because his friend's mom didn't know he was there. And the man in the reflective vest, Ray, the night attendant from the gas station across the road. Ray was about sixty. He had a grey ponytail and a laminated name badge and the tired, unshakable calm of a man who'd been robbed at gunpoint four times and considered it part of the job.

And me.

I'm going to be honest: if you'd asked any of them on day three who was in the party, I think they'd have said three people and then paused.

---

The first floor was a maze of stone corridors lit by torches that never burned down. It was full of things that had clearly been designed by somebody who'd heard of animals but never met one. There were rats with too many teeth. There was a kind of crab the size of a coffee table that screamed like a toddler when it was hit. Dana killed the first one with a fire extinguisher she'd got from a loot box. She did it in that dress, swearing at it in a professional, steady voice, the way I imagine she'd talked a drunk man off a gurney.

"Hold still, hold *still*, you son of a bitch, I'm trying to help you."

It wasn't helping it. But it held still eventually.

>
> **You killed a monster while wearing formal bridal attire! Your viewers have already given you a nickname. You're the Battle Bride now. I'm sorry. Nicknames are permanent. I don't make the rules. Well. I make most of the rules. Not that one.**

> **Reward: A Bronze Fan Box! It contains a veil. The veil has no stats. The viewers just really wanted you to have a veil.**

She wore the veil. Of course she wore the veil. She bunched it up and tucked it into her hair like a bandana, and after that the boxes kept coming. Fan Boxes, Sponsor Boxes, boxes from viewers with names that were just strings of symbols. She'd get three for killing a rat. Toby got one box a day, maybe. Ray got one for sitting down very calmly in a safe room and eating a sandwich while a monster beat on the door, which he said was the first time in his life anybody had paid him for that.

I didn't get any boxes. Not for a long time.

That's not a complaint. I want to be clear about that. I've never been so relieved about anything in my life.

---

Here's what I did, on the first two floors.

I fixed things.

My mother's shears were gone, and my ledger, and the steam iron, and the three-way mirror. But I had the pincushion on my wrist and the tape around my neck. I had a spool of white thread and a packet of needles in the pocket of my cardigan, because I always had a spool of white thread and a packet of needles in the pocket of my cardigan. And the dungeon kept giving us leather.

That's the thing nobody tells you. The loot boxes give you armour, but they don't give you armour that *fits*. You'd get a leather chest piece meant for someone with a torso like a filing cabinet, and it'd hang off Toby like a sandwich board. Or you'd get gauntlets cut for hands that didn't exist on Earth, with a slot for an extra thumb. Or you'd get a helmet with a crest on top that caught on every low ceiling and nearly broke Ray's neck twice.

So every night, in every safe room, I measured them.

I measured Toby's shoulders and his chest and the long, skinny reach of his arms, which were still growing. I measured Ray's head for the helmet. Then I took the crest off, because Ray said, very politely, that he'd sooner die without it. And I measured Dana, of course. I knew her measurements already. They were in the ledger, and the ledger was under several tons of strip mall. But I measured her again, every night, because her body was changing, because all our bodies were changing. We were walking twenty miles a day and eating rat.

I took everything in. I let everything out. I punched new holes in straps with the point of a crab claw. I lined the inside of Toby's chest piece with strips torn from the wedding train, the eleven satin loops and all, so it wouldn't rub his collarbones raw.

"You're like a pit crew," Toby said once, watching me work. "Like in NASCAR. Nobody watches the pit crew."

"Thanks, Toby."

"No, it's a compliment. The pit crew's the reason anybody *wins*."

He was sixteen. He meant it kindly. I don't think he knew he'd said the other part out loud.

On the second floor, Dana measured me.

We were in a safe room shaped like a train carriage, with brass fittings and seats that didn't recline and a little bar at one end. The bar served one drink, a sparkling purple thing that Ray said tasted like a grape had been told some bad news. Toby was asleep across three seats. Ray was asleep sitting up. Dana was on the floor with her back against the bar, still in the dress, which by then was the colour of a parking lot.

I'd just finished re-stitching her bodice. I'd finally had time to do it properly, with a hidden lapped seam that wouldn't pull when she swung the extinguisher.

"Your turn," she said.

"For what?"

She reached up and unhooked the tape measure from around my neck. Nobody had taken it off me in twenty-five years. I made a sound, a small one, like I'd been pinched.

"Arms up," said Dana.

"Dana. No."

"Katia, you've measured me about forty times. You've had your hands on my hips more than Kev has. Arms *up*." She had the nurse voice on. I'd heard her use it on rats. "I'm not going to write it down. I don't even have anything to write it down *on*."

I don't know why I let her. I think I was tired. I think it was the purple drink.

I put my arms up, and she wrapped the tape around me, and she read the number out loud.

It was just a number. I'd known it was coming. I knew roughly what it was, the way you know roughly what's in a letter from the bank. But I'd never heard anybody *say* it. I hadn't heard anybody say any number about me since I was fourteen. My mother had never needed to say it. She'd said it with her face.

I stood there with my arms up in a fake train carriage at the end of the world, and I started to cry. I hadn't cried at the collapse, or at the first rat, or when Toby took a crab claw through the thigh and I had to stitch the muscle back together with white thread while Dana held him down.

Dana didn't say anything kind. That was the kind thing. She just let the tape go slack and waited.

"It's a number," she said eventually. "I see a hundred people a night, Katia. Every one of them has a number. It's never once been the most important thing about them. It's usually about seventh."

"What's first?"

"Whether they're breathing." She shrugged. "After that it gets complicated."

She didn't give the tape back. She hung it around her own neck, over the dirty ivory, and struck a pose.

"Look at me," she said. "I'm a professional."

She was still wearing my tape measure two days later, when we found the room full of brides.

Ray saw it first. He had a sense for rooms. It was the same sense that had made him lock the gas station's door forty seconds before the guy with the gun came in, back in 2011. We came around a corner into a long, high gallery that looked like the inside of a department store. It had marble floors and gilt mirrors, and soft music playing from nowhere. It was lined, on both sides, with dressmaker's mannequins.

They were all wearing wedding dresses.

Fifty, sixty of them. Headless, smooth, stood in two neat rows facing the aisle, like guests waiting for somebody to walk down it. Every dress was different. Some were beautiful. Some looked like the dungeon had read about weddings in a magazine and got the wrong end of it entirely: dresses made of chain mail, dresses made of teeth.

At the far end of the gallery was the stairwell down to the third floor. It was lit up warm and gold, like an altar.

"No," said Ray. "Absolutely not."

"It's the stairs, Ray," said Toby.

"It's a *set*." Ray was right. I can see that now, from here, from the outside. Nobody builds a room like that for a rat. Somebody in a production office somewhere had a bride with an 81% approval rating, and the second floor was ending, and somebody had an idea.

The timer was nearly out. We didn't have time to go around. We went down the aisle.

Toby first, fast, with his spear. Then Ray. Then Dana, in the parking-lot dress, with the extinguisher on her shoulder and my tape around her neck. I came last, the way I always came last, with the bag of leather scraps and the thread.

We were halfway down when the music changed key.

The mannequins turned, all at once, with a sound like a thousand knuckles cracking. Every one of them had a face now, where no face had been. Every face was Dana's.

They didn't look at Toby. They didn't look at Ray. They went for the bride.

---

I want to tell you I fought.

I had a crab claw in my hand. I'd been using it to punch holes in straps. I could have used it for something.

What I did was stand still.

I didn't decide to. My body did it, the way it had done it on the fitting-room floor for twenty years: the knees soft, the shoulders down, the face blank. *I'm not here. I'm furniture. Don't mind me.* And the mannequins came pouring down the aisle from both sides, sixty of them, with Dana's face and Dana's freckles. They came right past me. One of them brushed my arm with a satin sleeve. Its head didn't even turn.

It looked straight through me.

They always did.

Ray dragged Toby the last twenty yards into the stairwell by his collar. Dana was swinging the extinguisher and shouting in her nurse voice, "Go, go, *go*, everybody go," and the white cloud of it went up around her like a veil. I lost sight of her in it. The mannequins closed over the place where she had been.

When the cloud settled, they all stood back in their two neat rows again, smooth and headless and facing the aisle, as if nothing had happened.

> **Crawler Dana Pruitt has died.**
>

>

I walked the rest of the aisle alone, in no hurry at all. Nothing in that room even turned its head.

She was on the floor at the bottom of the gilt mirror at the very end, near the stairs. There was nothing much left of her, in the way of a person. But the tape measure was still around her neck, the yellow-grey vinyl, the brass tip bent.

I knelt down and unwound it from her. My hands knew what to do.

In the mirror behind her there was a big woman in a filthy cardigan, kneeling, with a pincushion on her wrist. I looked at her for exactly one second.

Then I looked away, the way everyone did.
