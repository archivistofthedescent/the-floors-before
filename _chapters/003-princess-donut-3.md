I retired at the height of my powers.

That's how the great ones do it. An opera singer doesn't wait until her voice cracks. A queen doesn't wait for the peasants to bring the pitchforks. You leave while they still want you, and you let them spend the rest of their lives wondering what might have been.

After Spokane I did four more shows, purely as a courtesy. Then Miss Beatrice and I agreed, without either of us needing to say it out loud, that I'd given the circuit everything it deserved.

>
> The lavender curtains went on eBay the following week. So did the crown cushion. The silver glitter sign didn't sell. It's still in a closet. Or it was, until recently. Until there stopped being closets.

So we lived, Miss Beatrice and I, in the white apartment, like two great ladies in a villa after the war.

I need you to understand what she was like, because the System is going to tell you one version and I'm going to tell you another. Everybody else who met her only ever met her in passing. I *lived* with her. I'm the expert.

Miss Beatrice was always in motion. Even sitting down, she was moving: her foot jiggling, her thumb going on her phone, her eyes going round the room as if she'd lost something in it. She was going out, or she'd just come in, or she was about to go out again and was looking for her other shoe. She sang along to the radio in the car and never knew the words. She cooked once a month, something enormous and ambitious with a recipe on her phone, and gave up halfway through and ordered noodles instead.

She took my picture every day. Every single day. On the white couch, on the windowsill, in a little knitted crown she'd bought on the internet that I tolerated for the sake of the art. And she put the pictures up on her phone for my fans.

I had a great many fans.

There were also the parties.

Miss Beatrice had a lot of friends. They were loud and they smelled of perfume and limes, and they sat on the white couch, which nobody was allowed to sit on, except apparently them. When there was a party, she'd carry me into the bathroom with my bed and my water and shut the door.

"Just for tonight, baby," she'd say. "You'll hate them. Trust me."

I spent a great many nights on the bathroom tiles, listening to laughter through the door. Once, a man named Tyler came in to use the toilet, saw me, and said "oh, hey, it's the rug." I made sure he regretted this, in a way I'm not going to describe. I'll just say he had to go home early, and it was a long time before he wore those trousers again.

This next part I'm going to say only once.

Some nights, after the parties, after everyone had gone, Miss Beatrice didn't come to bed. She lay on the white carpet in the living room with all the lights off and her phone face down beside her, and she made a sound I hadn't heard a person make since Darlene's laundry room. When the kittens got taken.

I'd come out of the bathroom and walk across the dark and lie down against her side. Not on her. *Against* her. She'd put her hand in my fur and hold on like I was a rail on a boat.

"You're the only one who doesn't want anything from me," she said, one of those nights. "You know that? Everyone wants something."

That wasn't true. I wanted a great many things. I wanted the good food in the gold tin, and the windowsill in the afternoon, and for her not to drape the curtain over me any more, even though there wasn't a curtain any more.

But I let her think it. Some things you can give somebody just by keeping quiet.

She kept the rosette in a kitchen drawer, along with the takeout menus and the rubber bands and a set of keys that didn't open anything.

She'd tossed it in there the week we got back from Spokane. I knew it was there because I'd seen her do it, and I knew which drawer because I could smell it. It smelled of the ballroom, of chandeliers and mustard carpet, and faintly, under that, of the inside of Mrs. Kowalczyk's cardigan pocket.

Most afternoons I lay on the kitchen counter, directly above that drawer, in a patch of sun. Miss Beatrice thought I liked the counter because it was warm.

I let her think that too.

---

She met Carl in a hardware store.

She came home that day with a little paper bag and her face pink. "I needed a screw for the frame," she said, to me, to the phone, to nobody. "For your *picture*, baby. And there was this guy."

The picture was me. Of course it was me. It was the best photograph she'd ever taken: me on the white couch with the light coming in sideways, looking at the camera as though it owed me money. She'd had it printed huge and framed in gold, and it had been leaning against the wall for a month because she didn't know how to hang things.

The guy came over two days later to hang it.

He was very large. That was my first impression, and frankly, my second. He filled the doorway the way furniture does, and he ducked a little coming in, even though he didn't need to. He smelled of engine oil, salt water and cold air, the outside smell that people who work with their hands bring in on their clothes. He had boots on that sounded like somebody knocking politely on the floor.

He looked at me on my counter. I looked at him.

He didn't come over. He didn't make the noise people make at cats, the stupid *pspspsps*, like a tyre going flat. He didn't try to pick me up. He just nodded at me, once, the way you'd nod to someone you'd been introduced to at a funeral. Then he went and hung my picture.

He did it properly. He used a little level with a bubble in it. It took him fourteen minutes, and when he was done he stood back with his arms folded and looked at me, the real one, and then at the picture, and then at me again.

"She doesn't look like she likes me much," he said.

"She doesn't like *anyone*," said Miss Beatrice. "That's her whole thing."

I decided on the spot that he could stay. As staff.

I'll be quick about the next part, because nothing happened for a long time, and then everything did.

He came over. He fixed things. The drip in the bathroom tap, the drawer that stuck. He made eggs on Sundays. He sat on the *floor*. Do you understand how rare that is? Most humans in a room with a cat want to be above the cat. He'd sit with his back against the white couch and his legs out straight, reading something on his phone. He never once tried to make me come to him, so after a while I'd go and sit about a yard away from him, for the sake of the warmth.

"She's never done that," said Miss Beatrice, from the kitchen. She sounded pleased. And then, a little later, when she thought nobody was listening, she sounded something else.

Miss Beatrice got restless after that. More restless than usual. The phone calls got longer and went out on the balcony with the door shut. A suitcase came out from under the bed and lay open on top of it for a week, filling up a little at a time, like a tide coming in.

Then, one night, she made the binder.

She sat at the kitchen table until two in the morning with a label maker, a stack of plastic tabs and four different coloured pens. She went through everything. My food, my grooming, my vet, my show papers, my registry name. She made a tab for every one of them. The label maker went *chk-chk-chk* in the dark like a small, efficient insect.

I sat on the counter above the drawer and watched.

When she was nearly finished, she got up and opened the drawer. She took out the rosette, purple and silver, its tails crushed flat from a year under the takeout menus, and she looked at it for a long time.

"*Tenth*," she said.

She'd said it before. In the car, outside the hotel in Spokane, like something she'd dropped down a drain. This time it came out differently. Softer. Almost like a question.

Then she slid it into the plastic pocket inside the back cover of the binder, very carefully. She uncapped the red pen and wrote something small in the corner of the last page, and capped it again.

She left on a Tuesday, in the late morning, while Carl was at work.

I knew it was happening. Cats always know. The suitcase was zipped. She'd put on the sunglasses, on top of her head, even though it was grey out. She filled my water bowl to the brim, and then filled it again, though it was already full. She put three gold tins of the good food in a row on the counter, beside the binder, and wrote a note on a yellow square of paper and stuck it on top.

Then she crouched down in front of me.

I was on the kitchen floor by then. I'd been watching her the whole time. And at the very moment she crouched down, with her seashell fingernails reaching through the air towards my nose the way they'd reached through the wire of a plastic box a long time ago, I got up and turned around.

I faced the wall.

I'd like to tell you why. I'd like to tell you that I didn't know she was leaving, that I was bored, that I was making a point about the quality of the tins. I'd like to tell you anything else.

But you can't be turned away by someone you've already turned away from. That's logic. I learned it in a lavender cage.

"Okay," Miss Beatrice said behind me. Her voice went down, all the way down. "Okay, baby. Okay. Be good for him."

Then the door.

Carl came home at six. The apartment was dark, because Miss Beatrice had turned off all the lights before she left. She always did that, even when somebody was coming. He turned them on and saw the binder, and the tins, and the yellow note.

He read the note. Then he read it again. Then he stood in the kitchen for a long time with his big hands flat on the counter, reading it again, as though the words might rearrange themselves if he gave them enough chances.

They didn't. I could have told him that.

"Okay," he said eventually. To himself, I think. Then, louder, to the room: "Okay."

Then he opened the wrong tin.
